# Chapter 4 — The Rise and Fall of the Springfield Presbytery (1803–1804)

[← All chapters](.)

**The Story:** Barton W Stone and minister friends are brought up on trial. They withdrew, but refused to un-church the men who were un-churching them. When their own movement started becoming a sect, they killed it. In the process, they ascribed all religious authority to the gathered body of believers.

**The Idea**: Our movement is built on institutional self-sacrifice, and the willingness to lay down every obstacle to unity and freedom. And to relentlessly embrace the priesthood of all believers.

In June of 1804, a church body in Kentucky sat down and wrote its own will.

It was not dying. By its own account it was "in more than ordinary bodily health, growing in strength and size daily, and in perfect soundness and composure of mind." It had been alive for nine months. Its churches were multiplying. And its members had concluded that the best thing they could do for the church of Christ was to kill it.

To see why, go back nine months, to a garden in Lexington.

In September of 1803, the Synod of Kentucky — the governing body over every Presbyterian church in the state — met in Lexington. On its docket was Richard McNemar, a minister whose own presbytery in Ohio had already put him through what Barton Stone called "their fiery ordeal." His crime was preaching what the revival had taught a generation of frontier preachers: that the gospel was for everyone, and that anyone could respond to it. That was not what the Westminster Confession said, and the Confession was the standard every Presbyterian minister had sworn to.

Four other ministers sat in that Synod watching McNemar's case: Robert Marshall, John Dunlavy, John Thompson, and Barton Stone, the minister of Cane Ridge. They preached what McNemar preached. "It was plainly hinted to us," Stone remembered, "that we would not be forgotten by the Synod." When it became clear the verdict would go against McNemar, they knew it was going against all five of them.

In a short recess, the five walked out to a private garden. They prayed, they talked, and "with a perfect unanimity" they wrote two things: a protest against the Synod's handling of McNemar's case, and a declaration that they were withdrawing from the Synod's jurisdiction — *but not from its communion.*

That last clause is the whole story in miniature. They were leaving the court. They were not leaving the table. The men about to put them out of the church were still their brothers, and they would not put anyone out in return.

They handed the paper to the Moderator. "It was altogether unexpected by them," Stone wrote, "and a profound silence for a few minutes ensued."

The Synod sent a committee after them to win them back. It didn't go the way the Synod hoped: one of its members, Matthew Houston, was so persuaded by the five that he soon joined them. Another, the venerable David Rice, offered what Stone thought the argument most worth recording — that every step away from Calvinism was a step toward atheism. Calvinism to Arminianism, Arminianism to Pelagianism, Pelagianism to deism, deism to atheism. It did not move them.

So the Synod suspended them, and sent committees to read the "bull of suspension" from their pulpits and declare their churches vacant. Stone's answer was that the Synod had no more right to suspend men who had already withdrawn "than the pope of Rome had to suspend Luther, after he had done the same thing." But the damage was real: "not only were churches divided, but families." Stone looked at it and wrote: "not produced by the Bible; but by human authoritative creeds."

The five formed their own presbytery — the Springfield Presbytery — and published a book, the *Apology*, explaining their objections to every authoritative creed made by fallible men.

And then Stone did something that cost him personally. He called together his two congregations, Cane Ridge and Concord. He told them he could no longer preach to support the Presbyterian church, but would keep preaching among them for the kingdom, "irrespective of party." Then, in front of them, he tore up their salary obligation to him, so that no one would ever feel bound to pay him. "Never have I found a more loving, kind, and orderly people," he wrote of them. "This was truly a day of sorrow, and the impressions of it are indelible."

He had already freed the people he had once enslaved. Now he had no salary either. So he farmed. He preached almost every night, and often came home to find "the weeds were getting ahead of my corn," and worked his field at night while others slept. "I preferred honesty and a good conscience to all these things."

And it worked. The pamphlets written against them spread their views. People disgusted by the bitterness of the attacks came over to them. Churches were planted. Preachers joined.

Which is exactly when the problem began.

They had left the Synod because a party had put a creed between Christians. Now, as they grew, people began to see *them* as a party — and they began to feel like one. The witnesses who signed the will explained it plainly: they "endeavored to cultivate a spirit of love and unity with all Christians; but found it extremely difficult to suppress the idea that they themselves were a party separate from others. **This difficulty increased in proportion to their success.**"

Success was making them the thing they had protested. Some of their own people, the will says, were "wishing to make the Presbytery of Springfield their king." And when they sat down to write a book on church government, they found "neither precept nor example in the New Testament for such confederacies as modern Church Sessions, Presbyteries, Synods, General Assemblies." Their own presbytery was one of them.

So on June 28, 1804, sitting at Cane Ridge — the same place where three years before thousands had come through the mud to an open communion table — the Springfield Presbytery made its will.

> "We will, that this body die, be dissolved, and sink into union with the Body of Christ at large; for there is but one body, and one spirit, even as we are called in one hope of our calling."

Then it gave everything away. Its name, and the "Reverend" title, it willed to be forgotten, "that there be but one Lord over God's heritage, and his name one." Its power to make laws for the church, it willed to "forever cease." Its authority over preachers, it handed to the congregations: each church would "choose her own preacher… admit members… remove offences; and never henceforth delegate her right of government to any man or set of men whatever." To the people, it left the Bible. To its own followers who had wanted the presbytery as their king, it left a direction: "betake themselves to the Rock of Ages, and follow Jesus for the future." And to everyone, preachers and people alike: "cultivate a spirit of mutual forbearance; pray more and dispute less."

They took no name but *Christian*. They became, in Stone's words, "a by-word and laughing stock to the sects around; all prophesying our speedy annihilation." And yet, he wrote, "from this period I date the commencement of that reformation, which has progressed to this day."

The witnesses said the presbytery "was knit together in love, lived in peace and concord, and died a voluntary and happy death." They did it for "love to Christians of every name, the precious cause of Jesus, and dying sinners who are kept from the Lord by the existence of sects and parties in the church."

A will only takes effect when someone dies. That is the verse they put at the top of it: "where a testament is, there must of necessity be the death of the testator." The inheritance was the whole church of Christ, and it could only pass to the heirs once the presbytery was gone.

Five years later, in Pennsylvania, Thomas Campbell would write that the church is "essentially, intentionally, and constitutionally one," and that nothing but Christ can make it so. In Kentucky, a few men had already acted on it. They saw that their own institution — the one that was working — was starting to stand where only Christ should stand. And they let it die.

## Ideas

1. **Institutional self-sacrifice, in the name of unity.** Nine months old and thriving, the presbytery willed itself to "die, be dissolved, and sink into union with the Body of Christ at large." "It is appointed for all delegated bodies once to die."
2. **Authority returns to the gathered congregation.** Each church to "choose her own preacher… admit members… remove offences; and never henceforth delegate her right of government to any man or set of men whatever."
