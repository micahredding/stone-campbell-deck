Christendom’s Search for Unity

Card. Lands after the Preamble’s despair (“amid the diversity and rancor of party contentions”) and before “anywhere but in Christ.” One click per slide. Eight to ten minutes with every slide; 1690 and 1799 are marked cuttable in their notes.

325 · Council of Nicaea

Arians against non-Arians. A creed, and fifty-six more years of war.
Constantine had just reunited the empire (324) and found the church at war over whether the Son is God. He convened the council himself. The creed condemned Arius with the word homoousios.
Failure: Constantine was baptized on his deathbed by an Arian sympathizer; his son pushed the empire Arian; Athanasius was exiled five times. Jerome: “the whole world groaned and found itself Arian.”
→ The first creed written to unite the church did not unite the two sides; it named them.

381 · Council of Constantinople

Is the Spirit God too? The creed we still say, the Arians outlawed, and the Goths still Arian.
Theodosius made the Nicene faith imperial law (Cunctos populos, 380) and called the council (381). It finished the creed churches say today. Thirty-six Macedonian bishops, who denied the Spirit’s full divinity, walked out.
Failure: Arianism did not die; it left the empire. The Goths, Vandals and Lombards who took the West stayed Arian into the 500s and 600s. Canon 3 ranked Constantinople second after Rome, “because it is New Rome”: the seed of 1054.
→ The council finished the creed; the emperor made it law; the church that refused it moved across the border.

431 · Council of Ephesus

Does Mary bear God? A canon forbidding new creeds, and the Church of the East gone.
Nestorius objected to Theotokos; Cyril of Alexandria opened the council before John of Antioch arrived. Each side deposed the other’s leader, and the emperor arrested both. A Formula of Reunion patched it in 433.
Canon 7 forbade any “different faith” beside Nicaea’s: the ban the next council breaks, and the one the East cites against the West in 1054.
The Church of the East (Persia) refused Ephesus and has been separate since: today the Assyrian Church of the East.
→ Plant the canon. “We will come back to that.”

451 · Council of Chalcedon

One nature or two? A Definition, “not a new creed,” and Egypt gone.
Chalcedon reaffirmed Ephesus’s ban on new creeds, then issued the Definition (“in two natures”) and said it was a definition, not a creed. The 381 creed is first read into the record here. Egypt, Armenia, Ethiopia, the Syriac churches refused it: the Oriental Orthodox, non-Chalcedonian to this day (Copts, Armenians, Ethiopians, Eritreans, Syriacs, Malankara).
→ The rhyme to plant: “not a new creed.” The thirteen propositions will say the same words.',

482 · Instrument of Union

Zeno needs Egypt back. The Henotikon, and thirty-five years of schism with Rome.
Zeno’s Henotikon (“instrument of union”), drafted with Patriarch Acacius, tried to reconcile both sides by affirming Nicaea, Ephesus and Cyril and passing over Chalcedon in silence. It did not win Egypt back. Pope Felix III excommunicated Acacius (484), and Rome and Constantinople were out of communion until 519.
→ A document literally named “instrument of union” produced the first schism between Rome and Constantinople.

1054 · The Filioque

One word added to the creed. Legates sent to settle it, and East and West apart.
The Filioque (“and the Son”) entered the creed in Spain (Toledo 589), spread through the Frankish church, and was adopted at Rome about 1014. The East objected to the content and to the unilateral change, citing Ephesus Canon 7. Cardinal Humbert’s legation (1054) also contested leavened bread and papal primacy; on 16 July he laid the bull on the altar; Patriarch Cerularius anathematized the legates. The date is conventional; the estrangement was long and the 1204 sack sealed it. Mutual anathemas lifted 1965, communion not restored.
→ How do two churches with the same creed divide over the creed? Which version, and who decides.',

1521 · The Diet of Worms

Luther before the emperor. An edict to silence him, and the West in two.
1517 the theses. Rome answers with the bull Exsurge Domine (1520) and excommunicates Luther (3 January 1521). Charles V summons him to the imperial diet at Worms; he appears on 17–18 April 1521 and will not recant. The Edict of Worms (May 1521) declares him an outlaw and bans his writings. The instrument is the emperor’s edict, meant to hold the West together; it holds nothing, and the West is in two.
We come back to this room after the Preamble: Luther at Worms is the conscience thread.
→ An edict can outlaw a man. It cannot make him recant.

1527 · Zurich

No creed this time. The magistrate. A council ruling, the sword, and Felix Manz drowned.
The one instrument on the egg that was not a creed: unity by force. After the 1525 disputations, Zurich’s council ordered infants baptized and rebaptizers banished, then (March 1526) drowned. Felix Manz died 5 January 1527, the first Protestant executed by Protestants. Schleitheim (February 1527) set out believer’s baptism and separation from the sword; Sattler was burned that May.
Six years after Worms: the first split inside Protestantism. It comes back after Proposition 9.
→ Unity by creed fails; unity by force fails worse.

1529 · The Marburg Colloquy

The Lord’s Supper. Fourteen articles agreed, and “you have a different spirit.”
Marburg Colloquy, 1–4 October 1529, called by Philip of Hesse to unite the Protestants politically and theologically. The Marburg Articles: agreement on fourteen, and on the fifteenth (the Supper) agreement on everything except whether Christ’s body is bodily present. Luther’s line to the Swiss: “Ihr habt einen andern Geist.” Lutheran and Reformed have been separate confessional families since. The Formula of Concord (1577) settled Lutheran infighting by excluding the Reformed.
→ Thomas Campbell will call the Supper “that great ordinance of unity and love.” It is the one thing they could not agree on.',

1646 · The Westminster Confession

Three kingdoms at war. One confession for all three, and two thousand ministers ejected.
Solemn League and Covenant (1643): Scotland’s price for joining Parliament’s war was a common Presbyterian settlement for all three kingdoms. The Westminster Assembly (1643–49) produced the Confession (1646) and catechisms. The Independents (the “Dissenting Brethren”) argued against Presbyterian government inside the Assembly. Parliament never fully established it in England; the Restoration (1660) restored bishops; the Act of Uniformity (1662) ejected about two thousand ministers, the Great Ejection. Scotland adopted the Confession in 1647 and keeps it. The war came first; the Confession was written during it.
The pieces: the Church of England (bishops restored), the Independents, the English Presbyterians (ejected, 1662), the Church of Scotland. The Continental Reformed were never under Westminster and are not on this slide.
→ The confession Thomas Campbell was tried under was written to unite three kingdoms. It produced nonconformity.',

1690 · The Revolution Settlement

Scotland after the Revolution. Presbytery restored, and the Covenanters outside.
William and Mary’s settlement (1690) abolished bishops and established Presbyterianism with the Westminster Confession, but without the Covenants, which the state did not renew. The Cameronians (Society People) refused to enter and became the Reformed Presbyterian Church (1743 as a presbytery). The Episcopalians went out the other side.
Cuttable: the Secession slide carries the Scottish story on its own.',

1733 · The Secession

Patrons appoint ministers. Erskine rebuked, and four ministers walk out.
The Patronage Act (1712) restored lay patrons’ right to present ministers. Erskine’s synod sermon (October 1732) attacked it; the Assembly rebuked him (1733); he and three others (Wilson, Moncrieff, Fisher) protested, were suspended, and formed the Associate Presbytery (December 1733). Deposed 1740. The Seceders renewed the Covenants; the Week 2 litany note has this.
→ His tradition began as a protest against imposed authority.',

1747 · The Burgess Oath

May a Seceder swear it? A synod vote, and mutual excommunication.
The oath, required of burgesses in Edinburgh, Glasgow and Perth: “the true religion presently professed within this realm and authorized by the laws thereof.” Burghers: it means only “not Catholic.” Anti-Burghers: it blesses the church we left. The synod split April 1747 (Associate Synod vs General Associate Synod); the Anti-Burghers excommunicated the Burghers (1748–49), and the two could not take communion together. Thomas Campbell was Anti-Burgher. The Week 2 litany note covers why it felt so large (the Testimony, the Covenants).
→ This is the rule Conemaugh broke, and the room already knows it.',

1799 · 1806 · Old Light, New Light

May the magistrate enforce religion? Revised Testimonies, and each synod in two.
The Burghers split 1799 (Original Burghers, Old Light), the Anti-Burghers 1806 (Constitutional Associate Presbytery, Old Light) over whether the civil magistrate has power in religion (Westminster Confession ch. 23). Campbell sided with the Old Lights by friendship more than conviction (Foster; Week 2 note). He sailed April 1807.

…and today

Too many divisions to count.
The egg, shattered. Dozens of pieces, many of them named, the names chosen to make the room smile (four kinds of Church of Christ are in there). No click; it is simply on screen.
→ Every one of these was somebody’s instrument of unity.
The tree of divisions
Every branch is a church leaving another, placed at its year; time runs left to right, with the Reformation given most of the width. The leaves at the right edge are churches that still exist. Simplified and partial on purpose: the real tree does not fit on a wall.
→ Not one of these produced unity. Not one held even the people who signed it.
