The church as ekklēsia · a resource

The word Paul chose
was the name of the citizens’ assembly

A resource. In every Greek city Paul visited, ekklēsia was the regular meeting of the citizens: the body that heard, debated and voted. Paul named the churches with that word. Scholars now argue the democratic overtones were deliberate; others trace the word to the Greek Old Testament’s word for Israel’s assembly (qahal). Both are assemblies of the whole people. Every quote is in the document “The assembly.”
Athens: the open floor

“Freedom’s mark is also seen in this: Who hath wholesome counsel to declare unto the state?… What greater equality can there be in a city?”

Euripides, Suppliants (c. 423 BC), trans. Coleridge
• Theseus, defending democracy to a king’s herald. The line echoes what the herald cried at every Athenian assembly: Tis agoreuein bouletai?, “Who wishes to speak?” Any citizen could stand. Demosthenes remembers the morning Philip of Macedon took Elatea: “the marshal put the question, Who wishes to speak? No one came forward.” He calls that cry “the civic voice of our country.”
• All citizens over twenty could attend; votes were by show of hands. “Citizens” meant adult men only: women, slaves and foreigners were shut out.
The same word, in the New Testament

“…it shall be determined in a legal congregation… And when he had said these things, he dismissed the congregation.”

Acts 19:39–41 · The Living Oracles, Alexander Campbell’s New Testament (1826)
• The riot at Ephesus. The town clerk sends the mob home and points them to the city’s regular assembly. Luke calls both the mob and the lawful assembly ekklēsia. Campbell’s New Testament gives the civic assembly and the church one English word: congregation.
The scholars

“…Paul wished to portray his communities as alternative organizations existing alongside the civic assemblies… in many respects, the functioning of the Christian ekklēsia mirrors the operations of the civic assemblies.”

George H. van Kooten, New Testament Studies (2012)
• Van Kooten (Groningen, now Cambridge). Anna C. Miller, Corinthian Democracy (2015): the Corinthians understood themselves as “a powerful, democratic assembly.” Philip Esler, who thinks the word came from the Greek Old Testament, still says Paul’s churches took on the connotations of “civic voting assemblies.”
The Christian assembly

“When you are come together, each of you has a psalm, has a discourse, has a foreign language, has a revelation, has an interpretation!”

1 Corinthians 14:26 · The Living Oracles, Alexander Campbell’s New Testament (1826)
• Not a herald asking who wishes to speak: the Spirit has given every member something to bring. “To each is given the manifestation of the Spirit” (12:7).
The floor passes · 1 Corinthians 14:29–31
“…and let the others judge.”
“…you can all prophesy, one by one,”
“…that all may learn, and all may be comforted.”
The Living Oracles, Alexander Campbell’s New Testament (1826)
Three clicks. Speakers take turns; the gathered body weighs what is said. And the verse before: “if to another, sitting by, any thing be revealed, let the first be silent.” The floor passes. Caution: 14:34–35 silences women, against 11:5 where they pray and prophesy; many scholars think those verses were added later.
The assembly decides
“…this punishment, which was inflicted by the majority.” 2 Corinthians 2:6
“…who was also chosen by the congregations our fellow-traveller.” 2 Corinthians 8:19
The Living Oracles, Alexander Campbell’s New Testament (1826)
Two clicks. A vote, and an election. The Greek for “chosen” in 8:19 is cheirotonētheis, literally “by stretching out the hand”: the word for electing by show of hands. Esler: 2:6 “takes us straight into the dynamics of the political ekklēsia as a voting assembly.”
The translators’ fight

“…where I found this word ecclesia, I interpreted it by this word congregation.”

William Tyndale, answering Thomas More (1531)
• Tyndale’s reason: the clergy had taken for themselves “the term that of right is common unto all the whole congregation of them that believe in Christ.” More attacked him for it. King James’s rules for the 1611 translators, rule 3: “the Word Churche not to be translated Congregation.”
Campbell takes Tyndale’s side

“Wherever the word church is found in the common version, congregation will be found in this.”

Alexander Campbell, appendix to the Living Oracles
• His reason: “The term church, or kirk, is an abbreviation of the word kyriou oikos, the house of the Lord, and does not translate the term ekklēsia.” In 1824 he had printed Alexander Carson’s gloss: the Greeks, “particularly the Athenians,” used it “for their popular assemblies… in which none but citizens had a right to sit.”
Where authority comes from

“The community, the church, the multitude of the faithful, are the fountain of official power. This power descends from the body itself — not from its servants.”

Alexander Campbell, The Christian System (1839)
• From his chapter on the ministry. It continues: “How much surer and purer is ecclesiastic authority thus derived from Christ the head, immediately through his body, than when derived through a long, doubtful, corrupt dynasty of bishops or pontiffs!”

“Servants made by servants are servants of servants.”

Alexander Campbell, The Christian System (1839)
• The next sentence after “the fountain of official power.” Against ordination handed down through a line of clergy: office comes from the body, through Christ, not from the officers.
And back again

“…modern democracy began with the experience of the Puritan congregations.”

A. D. Lindsay, The Modern Democratic State (1943)
• A political philosopher (Master of Balliol, Oxford), seeing it from the other direction: the self-governing congregation as the seedbed of modern democracy. The ekklēsia took the city’s word; the city later learned from the congregation.

Not a building. Not a clergy.
The people, assembled.

The end of the resource. Ekklēsia names the gathering, every member with a voice, under Christ. → Back to the spine.
The Assembly Governs

The consent of all

Cyprian, Leo, Luther, the Orthodox, Rome
Every tradition remembers a time, or holds a principle, in which the whole people chose and consented.
The governance of all

“…I made up my mind to do nothing on my own private opinion, without your advice and without the consent of the people.”

Cyprian, bishop of Carthage, c. 250
• A bishop of the third century, to his clergy, on how he had governed from the beginning.

“He who is to govern all, should be chosen by all.”

Leo the Great, bishop of Rome, Letter 10 (444)
• The next sentence: “The consent of the clergy, the testimony of those held in honour, the approval of the orders and the laity should be required.” A pope.
Luther, 1523 · for the congregation at Leisnig
“…the sheep are to judge whether what they teach is the voice of Christ or the voice of strangers.”
“All teachers should and must, therefore, be subject with their teaching to the judgment of the hearers.”
Martin Luther, That a Christian Assembly or Congregation Has the Right and Power to Judge All Teaching and to Call, Appoint, and Dismiss Teachers (1523)
Two clicks. Leisnig had dismissed the priest its patron appointed and elected its own. Luther: “If the bishop does not confirm him, he is none the less confirmed by virtue of the call of the congregation.” Later he settled for princes running the church. Our preachers answer to the congregation: this is why.
Luther, 1523 · a witness

“…no one can deny that every Christian has God’s Word and is taught of God and anointed by Him to the priesthood.”

Martin Luther, That a Christian Assembly or Congregation Has the Right and Power to Judge All Teaching and to Call, Appoint, and Dismiss Teachers (1523)
• The ground of the congregation’s right: every baptized Christian is a priest.
Luther, 1523

“If the bishop does not confirm him, he is none the less confirmed by virtue of the call of the congregation.”

Martin Luther (1523)
• Luther wrote it and later settled for princes running the church. Brush Run, three centuries on, did it.
Orthodox

“…the protector of religion is the very body of the Church, even the people themselves…”

Encyclical of the Eastern Patriarchs (1848)
• The four Orthodox patriarchs, answering Pius IX: neither patriarchs nor councils could introduce novelties, because the people guard the faith. And at every Orthodox ordination the people still shout Axios!, “He is worthy!”, the trace of the time when the whole church chose.
Catholic

“…in this Church, as in an inverted pyramid, the top is located beneath the base. Consequently, those who exercise authority are called ‘ministers’, because, in the original meaning of the word, they are the least of all.”

Pope Francis, October 17, 2015
• The same address quotes “a principle dear to the Church of the first millennium”: quod omnes tangit ab omnibus tractari debet, what touches all should be discussed by all.
Why it is beautiful

“Juridical power is a substitute for love in actual social life… In the Church, where perfect love dwells, there is no need for such a substitute.”

Nicolas Afanasiev, The Church of the Holy Spirit, p. 274
• The case for congregational governance is not a legal one. Where love governs, the apparatus of power is unnecessary.
Church government · a resource

Who governs the church?

What Jesus said, what Cane Ridge willed,
and what Brush Run did.
A resource, to take when the room asks. The sources run in order: Matthew 18, the priesthood texts, the Declaration and Address on Matthew 18, the Witnesses’ Address and the Last Will, Alexander Campbell.
To the gathered church

“…tell it unto the church: but if he neglect to hear the church, let him be unto thee as an heathen man and a publican. Verily I say unto you, Whatsoever ye shall bind on earth shall be bound in heaven…”

Matthew 18:17–18 (KJV)
• In Matthew 16:19 the keys go to Peter. Here the binding and loosing go to “the church”: the gathering that hears a brother’s trespass. The next verses: two who agree; two or three gathered.

“For where two or three are gathered together in my name, there am I in the midst of them.”

Matthew 18:20 (KJV)
• The Declaration and Address quotes it to the friends of reform: “Let not the paucity of your number… prove an insuperable discouragement.”
Thomas Campbell on Matthew 18 · Appendix

“‘What ye bind on earth shall be bound in heaven’ is the awful sanction of the Church’s judgment, in justly rejecting any person. Take away this, and it has no sanction at all.”

Thomas Campbell, Declaration and Address (1809)
• Matthew 18:18 gives the church’s verdict heaven’s weight. So to cast someone out over an opinion is to pronounce him lost: “Does he not henceforth become to us as a heathen man and a publican?” The weight is why the church must not spend it on opinions.

“An individual church or congregation of Christ’s disciples is the only ecclesiastical body recognized in the New Testament. Such a society is ‘the highest court of Christ’ on earth.”

Alexander Campbell, Christian Baptist, July 5, 1824
• From “Essays on Ecclesiastical Characters, Councils, Creeds, and Sects, No. III.” Every session, presbytery, synod, assembly or conference that legislates or rules “essentially becomes ‘the man of sin and the son of perdition.’” He then reprints Alexander Carson on Matthew 18: “Here the last appeal is to the church. He does not say, If he does not hear the church, take him to the presbytery…”
Every believer
“But ye are a chosen generation, a royal priesthood, an holy nation, a peculiar people…” 1 Peter 2:9 (KJV)
“And hath made us kings and priests unto God and his Father…” Revelation 1:6 (KJV)
Two clicks. Said of the whole church, not of an order within it. Richardson’s note on Alexander’s reply cites both.

“…they soon found that there was neither precept nor example in the New Testament for such confederacies as modern Church Sessions, Presbyteries, Synods, General Assemblies, etc.”

The Witnesses’ Address, appended to the Last Will and Testament, 1804
• Researching a book on church government, the Springfield Presbytery found that it was itself one of these confederacies; it dissolved itself.
The will’s bequests on government
To the church: “assume her native right of internal government… and admit no other proof of their authority, but Christ speaking in them.”
To each congregation: “choose her own preacher… and never henceforth delegate her right of government to any man or set of men whatever.”
To the ministers: “obtain license from God to preach the simple Gospel…”
Last Will and Testament of the Springfield Presbytery, 1804
Three clicks. Cane Ridge, 1804. “Native”: it was always hers.

“Why do we contend for uninterrupted succession in ordination, seeing it is not the persons called bishops who have the power, but the people?”

Alexander Campbell · in Richardson, Memoirs, sermon on Titus 1:5
• Brush Run, winter 1811–12. The same sermon: ordination “consists in the choice of the people, which must be hearty.”

“As to the form or ceremony of ordination, he did not regard it as conferring any authority, but as a public testimony that the persons ordained possessed the necessary authority.”

Robert Richardson, Memoirs of Alexander Campbell (1868)
• Richardson’s summary of Alexander’s view, which he says “he continued to maintain unchanged through life.” Ordination recognizes; it does not confer.

Christ in the midst of two or three.
Every other office answers to that.

Confession or constitution · a resource

What governs the church?

The Westminster Confession, and the Declaration and Address
A resource, split from “Church government.” Two answers: a confession that puts the keys in the hands of officers, and Thomas Campbell’s New Testament as the church’s whole constitution, with many local congregations and one church.
The other answer · Westminster Confession, chapter 30
30.1 “The Lord Jesus, as King and Head of his church, hath therein appointed a government, in the hand of church officers…”
30.2 “To these officers the keys of the kingdom of heaven are committed…”
Westminster Confession of Faith, American text (1788)
Two clicks. Of Church Censures. Matthew 18 gives the binding and loosing to the church; Westminster gives the keys to its officers. Richardson: Thomas Campbell, applying to the Synod of Pittsburg, accepted the Confession “with the exception that… [he] objected to the chapter conferring power upon the clergy.”
Proposition 4

“…the New Testament is as perfect a constitution for the worship, discipline, and government of the New Testament Church… as the Old Testament was for the worship, discipline, and government of the Old Testament Church…”

Thomas Campbell, Declaration and Address (1809)
• The constitution is the New Testament. Nothing written since governs the church.
Proposition 2

“That although the Church of Christ upon earth must necessarily exist in particular and distinct societies, locally separate one from another, yet there ought to be no schisms, no uncharitable divisions among them.”

Thomas Campbell, Declaration and Address (1809)
• Local congregations, many; one church. The locality is natural; the division is the sin.

Not a confession over the church.
The New Testament is its constitution.

Against succession · a side path

“Why do we contend for uninterrupted succession in ordination, seeing it is not the persons called bishops who have the power, but the people?”

Alexander Campbell, sermon on Titus 1:5 · in Richardson, Memoirs
Side path: the argument with the alternatives, for when the room asks. No apostolic succession, no line of hands from Peter to now that makes a minister. The power is in the people.
Richardson: he did not regard ordination “as conferring any authority, but as a public testimony that the persons ordained possessed the necessary authority.”
Asked why he preached without authority
“Who has authority?
Who gave the Presbytery authority to license men?
Who gave the Presbytery authority to make laws for the Church?
Who gave the Presbytery authority to decide religious matters by vote?
Who gave the Presbytery authority to choose ministers?”
Alexander Campbell · in Richardson, Memoirs
Four clicks; the question keeps extending. End of the side path. He turns the charge around: the presbytery is the one without authority.

The assembly chooses.
Christ governs.

The end. The congregation is the fountain of office; Christ is its head; every other office answers to that.
