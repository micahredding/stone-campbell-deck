## 1. Universalism, a movement of its own

**What to say:** by 1828 Universalism was not a stray opinion. It was a denomination, with preachers, conventions, papers and a confession of faith, and it was growing fast on the Western Reserve.

**Sources.** The research texts are in the database at `91 Extracted Text/Universalism Sources (2026-11-24)/`:
- Eddy, *Universalism in America* (1884–86), cited **Eddy**;
- Whittemore, *Modern History of Universalism* (1830);
- Winchester, *Dialogues* (1792, 1803 and 1831 editions);
- Ballou, *Treatise on Atonement*;
- Hayward, *Religious Creeds and Statistics* (1836).

**Founders and documents:**
- **John Murray** reached America in 1770. His first sermon was at Good Luck, New Jersey. The church at Gloucester, Massachusetts adopted its articles in 1779 (Eddy I).
- **Elhanan Winchester**, *Dialogues on the Universal Restoration* (London, 1788). This is the book that converted Raines.
  - "Christ is the great attracting loadstone, that shall finally draw all things to him" (1831 ed., exact).
  - His stance foreshadows Raines at Warren: "I have always made it a rule never to press the belief of my sentiments upon my friends… I never have refused to own my sentiments, when asked" (1831 ed., pp. 53–54; OCR).
- **The Winchester Profession, 1803.** This is the General Convention's confession. Article II: "We believe that there is one God, whose nature is Love, revealed in one Lord Jesus Christ, by one Holy Spirit of Grace, who will finally restore the whole family of mankind to holiness and happiness" (Eddy II, 49; exact).
  - On the same page is a Universalist parallel to Campbell's faith/opinion line: "where the brethren cannot see alike, they may agree to differ" (Eddy II, 50; OCR).
- **Hosea Ballou**, *Treatise on Atonement* (1805): "the atonement by Christ was the effect, and not the cause, of God's love to man" (p. 99).

**Two kinds of Universalist:**
- From 1817, Ballou taught **no punishment after death**. His opponents called it "death and glory."
- **Restorationists**, like Winchester and Raines, held a real punishment after death that ends in restoration.
- In 1831 the Restorationists split off at Mendon, Massachusetts (Paul Dean, Adin Ballou and others). They resolved that "No-Future accountability… [is] subversive of the best interests of society" (Eddy II, 331; OCR).
- Raines kept the same line all his life: "I make a distinction between Restorationism and Universalism" (H p. 170).

**Size:**
- By 1836, about 317 preachers and 653 societies, with a claimed "population" of about 500,000 (Hayward 1836, p. 151; the OCR of the table is garbled, so the figures are approximate).

**On the Western Reserve:**
- **The first preacher.** New Englanders brought it west. In 1814, Timothy Bigelow came to Ohio from Winchester, New Hampshire, because his old neighbors there wanted "a Universalist Minister to break up the ground" (Eddy II, 126).
- **The Association.** At Palmyra, Portage County, in 1821, eleven churches and sixteen societies formed the **Northern Ohio Universalian Association**. They ordained **Ebenezer Williams** "minister at large" (Eddy II, 393; Whittemore pp. 427–428). He is the friend Raines later baptizes at Sandy Lake.
- **Its growth, in Bigelow's words that year:** "your whole number, seven years ago, who believed in the universal love of God, did not exceed twenty; and at this time, on the same ground, your numbers are more than fifteen hundred" (Whittemore p. 428; exact).
- Hayden: "Restorationists, who were numerous" (H p. 130). At Austintown "the Universalists… composed the larger part of the assembly" (H p. 131).

**Why the orthodox shunned it.** It was accused of licensing sin and dissolving social order (Encyclopedia of the Stone-Campbell Movement, "Universalism"). Winchester's own objector puts it this way: "One grand objection… is, that it tends to licentiousness" (*Dialogues*, 1831 ed., p. 147; OCR). For a Baptist association to receive a Universalist preacher was unheard of.

**Raines's road in:**
- **Birth.** Born near Fredericksburg, Virginia. Hayden gives 1797. His gravestone, as reported on a website, gives January 22, 1798, which still needs checking.
- **Childhood.** At four, he is "christened" in the Episcopal church, in "tears… of fear and apprehension" (H p. 150; Hayden's words).
- **Kentucky.** At 14 the family moves to Jefferson County, Kentucky.
- **Doubt.** Contradictory preaching and Paine's *Age of Reason* leave him "skeptical."
- **Conversion.** Teaching near Fredonia, Indiana, he loses arguments with his Restorationist employers, and Winchester's *Dialogues* "completed the work" (H p. 151; Hayden).

**Raines (1868):** "I got religion. The sky appeared to be bluer, the leaves looked greener, and the birds sang more sweetly than ever before." (H p. 151, exact)

**Raines (1868):** He preached Restorationism "five years" (H p. 151; the word is split across a line in the scan). In the last two years he travels "at large," and that brings him to the Western Reserve.

**His later life** (notes): over fifty years of ministry in Ohio and Kentucky, longest at Paris, Kentucky. He died at Eminence, Kentucky, on September 7, 1881. That date is from a website citing his gravestone; check it before it goes on a slide.

## 2. The geography

**The Western Reserve.**
- **Origin.** Connecticut kept this strip along Lake Erie when it ceded its western claims in 1786. It sold the land in 1795, and Moses Cleaveland surveyed it in 1796. It became part of Ohio in 1800, as Trumbull County.
- **Counties by 1828:** Trumbull, Ashtabula, Geauga, Portage, Cuyahoga, Medina, Lorain and Huron.
- **Settlers.** New Englanders, which is why Universalism was strong there.

## 3. Windham: he came to argue (spring 1828)

1. **The scene.** The Reserve is in an uproar over Scott. Raines (1868): "Misrepresentations… were as numerous as blackbirds in August." (H p. 152)
2. **The plan.** He hears Scott calls for objections after each sermon, so "I let a number of my brethren know that I intended to oppose him." (H p. 152)
3. **The first night.** Scott reads 1 Corinthians 1 and "preached it through… the six points of the gospel."
   - Raines (1868): "when he called for objections I was confounded. I could see the heads of my brethren moving to the right and left, in the crowd, expecting to see me rise to my feet. But they didn't see me rise!" (H p. 152)
4. **Outside, in the yard.** His brethren ask: "Bro. Raines, what do you think of the discourse?" He answers: "I can do nothing against the gospel as preached by Bro. Scott." (H p. 153, OCR)
   - Raines (1868): "I understood the first I ever heard a great deal better than I desired." (H p. 153, exact)
5. **The second night: the Scriptures come alive.** Scott preaches on 1 Corinthians 15, the resurrection.
   - Raines (1868): "Germs of truth, and beauties and glories sprang from the bosom of that chapter under the handling of Bro Scott, of which before I had scarcely any conception." (H p. 153)
   - "As in Adam all die, even so in Christ shall all be made alive" had been his own proof-text. He had read it as a "scrap", without the chapter around it.
   - Richardson: Scott "was accustomed to take the Scriptures connectedly" (R p. 221, exact).
6. **The third night: the two covenants.** Raines (1868): "although I had been a preacher five years, I certainly did not know the difference between the old covenant and the new. I obtained from them a sort of hotch-potch; or rather I made of them a chaos, and preached the darkness that was on the face of the deep!" (H p. 153, exact)
7. **The fourth night: Hebrews 11, the story of faith.** "Here I virtually surrendered — not that I was convinced that all men would not be finally saved." (H p. 154)
   - Scott said nothing against his opinion, "only that it was a philosophy, as was Calvinism, Arminianism, etc., and no part of Christianity." (H p. 154)
8. **He keeps his old appointments.** Raines (1868): "I told my old brethren that I threw myself on their mercy; in other words, that if they believed me to be going astray, in mercy to set me right." (H p. 154)
9. **Austintown, April (1828 inferred; Hayden gives no year).** He preaches his renunciation (Hayden's report, H pp. 130–131):
   - "My friends, I find myself in a strait… I can see no way out, with the Word of God in my hand, but through the obedience of faith in baptism. If any of you can see any other, I implore you in the name of my Master to show it to me."
   - The Universalists in the crowd "jerked up their chairs, started over the benches, and hurried to their homes."
10. **Sandy Lake, May 1828.** He goes to his friend Ebenezer Williams, a Restorationist preacher in Ravenna: "he had been hearing Walter Scott; that he had got into trouble, and wanted me to help him out." (Williams, H p. 156)
    - Williams: "taking hold of each other's hands, we walked into the water. I baptized him; he in turn baptized me… Some cried, some scolded." (H p. 157)
    - Williams pays for it: "Thus I turned my back on the four hundred a year."
11. **The result.** "within five weeks I immersed fifty persons, three of them, counting Bro. Williams, talented Restorationist preachers." (Raines, H p. 154)

## 4. On the road with Thomas Campbell (summer 1828)

- Raines (1868): "He interested himself in my favor, and had me travel and preach with him several months… By day and by night, publicly and from house to house, he was my teacher." (H pp. 149–150, exact)
- "Often have I seen him, when he had no reason to believe that any eye saw him… in his closet, prostrate on his face, pouring out his soul in prayer to God." (H p. 150)
- Thomas: "Bro. Raines and I have been much together for the last several months, and we have mutually unbosomed ourselves to each other." (Raines's memory of Thomas's words, H p. 168)
- An old Calvinist and a young Universalist, travelling and preaching together. They disagree, and Thomas never tries to argue him out of it.

## 5. Warren: the trial (last week of August 1828)

1. **The worry.** The disciples feared Raines and Williams were "merely baptized Universalists. If this was all, it was nothing." (Hayden, H p. 159) There were "out-of-door discussions touching the prudence of such a loose proceeding" (H p. 163).
2. **His frame of mind.** Raines (1868): "I went to that association expecting trouble… But I resolved to breast the storm." (H p. 167)
3. **Friday: Alexander's sermon.** It is Raines's first time hearing Alexander Campbell. The text is Romans 14:1, "receive ye, but without regard to differences of opinion."
   - Raines: "I would have at least Bro. Campbell on my side." (H p. 167)
4. **Saturday morning, in the street.** Dr. Wright stops him: "I understand that you sometimes tell people that you still believe that all men will finally become holy and happy."
   - "I do, sir." "What then will you do with this passage: *These shall go away into everlasting punishment*…?"
   - "I will not do any thing with it… If I argue with you in defense of my opinions I shall make myself a factionist. But… if you get up an argument with me, be careful, you will make yourself a factionist."
   - The doctor: "Well, sir, I'll see whether this association will fellowship men of your views." (H pp. 167–168, exact)
   - Raines: "I will show you that I will have Thomas Campbell, A. Campbell, Walter Scott, Bentley, and a number of others on my side." "It is impossible." "Well, try it."
   - The accuser's name is Wright. Micah may enjoy that before the turn to the other Wright.
5. **Saturday afternoon: the case.** Jacob Osborne, "very lugubriously", brings it before the Association.
6. **Thomas Campbell stands** (Raines's memory, H p. 168): "The devil has brought this question into this association to sow discord among brethren… I am a Calvinist, and he a Restorationist; and, although I am a Calvinist, I would put my right arm into the fire and have it burnt off before I would raise my hand against him. And if I were Paul, I would have Bro. Raines in preference to any other young man of my acquaintance to be my Timothy."
7. **Scott:** "I think that Bro. Raines has been very badly treated, and I fear that when he speaks he will speak with too much severity." (H p. 168)

## 6. Alexander's proposal

1. **Saturday afternoon.** Alexander repeats the sermon's point, the difference between faith and opinion.
   - Richardson's summary: Raines's view "could never be considered a matter of belief, since there was no testimony to render it such" (R p. 245, exact).
   - The proposal: preach "the gospel as the apostles preached it, and… retain his opinions as private property" (R p. 245).
   - Campbell, in his own words: "if it be a wrong private opinion, it will die a natural death much sooner than if you attempt to kill it" (MH p. 147).
2. **On the bench.** "Bro. Campbell requested me to stand upon a bench, and proclaim to the large concourse present, my own views… That my Restorationism was a philosophy. That I would neither preach it nor contend for it." Hayden's footnote: the bench was "the better to be heard, the house being very full." (H pp. 168–169)
3. **The question.** "Whether there was any law of Christ by which I could be condemned?" The answer was "in my favor by an overwhelming majority." (H p. 169)
4. **The sunrise ambush.** Next morning, at the prayer meeting, an old brother moves to disfellowship anyone who denies "that sinners are saved by grace."
   - Raines: "In a twinkling I was on my feet, and said: 'I second that motion; for by grace are ye saved through faith…'"
   - "The old Brother who had put the motion, struck a direct line for the door, and the congregation followed him; and there my association troubles ended." (H p. 169, exact)
5. **How it ended.**
   - Raines (1868): "Had they attempted to brow-beat me I might have been ruined forever. But treating me kindly, at the same time that they convinced me that my opinion, whether true or false, dwindled into nothingness in comparison with the faith of the gospel, redeemed me." (H p. 169)
   - "the opinion faded, and in ten months was numbered with all my former errors." (H pp. 169–170)
   - Raines, writing in 1830: "The facts of the New Testament will conquer the world. They have conquered me…" and "if I were Timothy, father Campbell should, in preference to any man, be my Paul." (MH pp. 148–149)
   - Campbell: "in the same proportion as individuals labor to be of one opinion, they disagree." (MH p. 149)

**Honest notes:**
- The terms were that he would not teach his opinion.
- A minority nearly split the Association, and some would-be members walked away (R p. 246).
- Hayden says the bench speech and the vote were "a marked victory for the truth" (H p. 170); he was a partisan eyewitness.

## 7. The hinge: what way of reading made it come alive?

- **What Raines saw:** Scott read whole chapters "connectedly" and set the covenants in order. He told the story of faith (Hebrews 11).
- **What Raines had done before:** he made a "chaos" of the covenants and used a "scrap" of 1 Corinthians 15 as a proof-text.
- **Alexander's sermon at Warren gave the theory** (Hayden's summary, H pp. 164–165). There are three kinds of thing:
  - knowledge: what the apostles saw and "handled";
  - faith: "Faith stands on testimony. No testimony, no faith." (exact);
  - opinion: everything else, "the fruitful source of all the schism."
- **The question for class:** is that what made 1 Corinthians 15 come alive for Raines? Or was it something Campbell's categories don't quite name: the story, the covenants told in order, a chapter read as a whole?
- **The turn to Wright.** Scripture as a story in acts. The Old Testament has "the authority that an earlier act of the play would have" (Laing Lecture, p. 19). Raines's "two covenants" lesson is Wright's earlier and later acts.
  - Raines read a "scrap" and missed the plot. Scott read the chapter and the plot came alive.

## A contrast, not yet placed (2026-11-25)

- **Stacy and Person, New York, 1817–18** (Eddy II, pp. 7–8). Cornelius G. Person, a Universalist preacher, asked to be immersed: "neither would baptize him unless he would renounce his faith in Universal Salvation" (Baptist and Methodist both). A fellow Universalist, Nathaniel Stacy, immersed him through the ice.
- The point: the Baptists and Methodists asked him to give up the opinion first. The Mahoning Association, itself still Baptist in 1828, received Raines with his opinion kept as private property.
- In `slides.py` as a hidden slide just before "The worry" (Warren). Other candidate places: Sandy Lake; before Alexander's proposal.
- Why the Universalists resisted the Disciples' baptism: its necessity and mode, not baptism itself. Williams: "baptism was but voluntary and quite unessential"; "I had been sprinkled — I had sprinkled others" (H pp. 155–156).
