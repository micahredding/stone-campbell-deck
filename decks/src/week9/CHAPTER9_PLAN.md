## The hook: Cross Creek, August 30, 1816

Source: Richardson, *Memoirs* I, pp. 469–483. Campbell's own retrospect is in MH 1846, p. 493. Both are checked in the database texts.

1. **The scene.** The Redstone Baptist Association meets at Cross Creek, Virginia. On the way there, Campbell tells his wife: "I do not think they will let me preach at this Association at all."
2. **The maneuver.** Elder Pritchard, whose church is three miles away and who resents the new Wellsburg meeting-house, invokes a "Maryland rule" to keep Campbell off the Lord's-day list. He says, "the people can hear him at any time."
3. **The people insist.** David Phillips comes the next morning: a large number want Campbell to preach. Then the substitute, Elder Stone, falls ill. At the bridge, Pritchard himself has to say it: "you must preach to-day."
4. **The sermon.** It is preached in the open air, under the elms and plane trees by the creek, on Romans 8:3: "what the law could not do…"
5. **The disruption.** A lady faints, and Pritchard comes into the stand and "created great disturbance… as if with a design of preventing the people from hearing."
6. **The verdict.** At the intermission Pritchard says: "This will never do. This is not our doctrine. We cannot let this pass…" Elder Estep answers: "It is better to let it pass and let the people judge for themselves." This touches the class themes of freedom and the priesthood of the people.
7. **The aftermath.** In 1817 Campbell is tried for heresy, and is saved "by a great stretch of charity on the part of two or three old men." Then come "a seven years' war". Campbell, in 1846, doubted that "but for the persecution… the present reformation had ever been advocated by me."

**Why it is the right hook.** The heresy was a *hermeneutic*. Campbell said the Bible is not one flat rulebook. It comes in ages: Patriarchal, Jewish, Christian. The law of Moses was an earlier one, given "to the Jewish nation and to none else". "Might we not as reasonably say, that every man must be at once a Patriarch, a Jew, and a Christian…?" So Old Testament arguments for infant baptism, tithes, holy days, and an established church "fall ineffectual to the ground" (MH 1846, pp. 507–520).

173 years later, Wright makes the same move: "The Old Testament has the authority that an earlier act of the play would have, no more, no less" (Laing Lecture, 1989, p. 19).

**Hook alternative, if wanted.** Washington, Pa., 1809: Andrew Munro tells Thomas Campbell that the new motto ends infant baptism. That is a hermeneutic's first consequence, but it is less of a scene. It is not yet checked in the database.

6. **The fork: what do you do in the last act?**
   - Campbell restores the first scene: the "Ancient Order" series, CB 1825, "discard… every thing that is not found written".
   - Wright improvises the next scene in character, with "no implicit command that the actors should repeat the earlier parts."
   - Campbell's own fences belong here: expediency for "circumstantials" (CS pp. 95–96); inference never a test (D&A Prop. 6); "the image of Christ", not exactness "in a few items" (Lunenburg, 1837).
7. **The other difference: what a fact is.**
   - Campbell: the same rules should give "uniformity in opinion" (MH 1846, p. 13).
   - Then Lexington, February 1832, the callback to Chapter 8. They pledged the New Testament "alone" and split within a week over who may serve the loaf.
   - Wright: "the more that you insist that you are based on the Bible, the more fissiparous you become" (Laing, p. 13).
8. **What his heirs kept.** The pattern half hardened (command, example, inference), and the divisions to come follow from it: Lipscomb, the instrument, the "one cup".
   - Wright gives back the half of Campbell that his heirs dropped: the story.

**Candidate ideas:**
- The Bible is a story, not a rulebook. (Campbell said so first.)
- We live in its last act, which is not yet finished.
- Reading the story together keeps us together; agreeing on rules does not.
- "Let the people judge for themselves."
