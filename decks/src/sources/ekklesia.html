The church as ekklēsia, the assembly: the Greek citizens’ assembly, Paul’s word for the church, its translators, and Alexander Campbell on where the church’s authority comes from. Each excerpt was read in the source named (October 2026); the full dossier, with links, is in the research database (micahredding/stone-campbell-database, 10 Research Projects/Ekklesia and Greek Democracy).

Euripides, Suppliants 438–441 (c. 423 BC) · trans. E. P. Coleridge (Perseus)

Freedom’s mark is also seen in this: Who hath wholesome counsel to declare unto the state? And he who chooses to do so gains renown, while he, who hath no wish, remains silent. What greater equality can there be in a city?

Coleridge’s note: A reference to the question put by the herald in the Athenian ἐκκλησία, Τίς ἀγορεύειν βούλεται; It here serves as a marked characteristic of democracy.

Demosthenes, On the Crown 170 (330 BC) · trans. C. A. and J. H. Vince (Perseus)

As soon as he had told his tale, the marshal put the question, Who wishes to speak? No one came forward. The marshal repeated his question again and again, but still no one rose to speak… for we may justly regard the voice, which the crier raises as the laws direct, as the civic voice of our country.

Mogens Herman Hansen, “The Concepts of Demos, Ekklesia, and Dikasterion in Classical Athens,” GRBS 50 (2010)

All citizens over twenty were admitted to the ekklesia. (p. 531)

In the assembly the vote was by show of hands; in the court it was by ballot. (p. 533)

Appendix, on Matthew 16:18: Wherever the word church is found in the common version, congregation will be found in this.

The term church, or kirk, is an abbreviation of the word κυρίου οἶκος, the house of the Lord, and does not translate the term ἐκκλησία.

George H. van Kooten, “Ἐκκλησία τοῦ θεοῦ: The ‘Church of God’ and the Civic Assemblies,” New Testament Studies 58.4 (2012) · abstract

I argue that its Graeco-Roman political meaning in the sense of ‘civic assembly’ was decisive in its adoption by Paul, and that Paul wished to portray his communities as alternative organizations existing alongside the civic assemblies… in many respects, the functioning of the Christian ἐκκλησία mirrors the operations of the civic assemblies.

Anna C. Miller, “Not with Eloquent Wisdom: Democratic Ekklēsia Discourse in 1 Corinthians 1–4,” JSNT 35.4 (2013) · abstract; and Corinthian Democracy (2015) · publisher’s description

…to establish his authority by negotiating with a community that understood itself as a powerful, democratic assembly.

…an authoritative democratic assembly in which leadership and ‘citizenship’ cohered with the public speech and discernment open to each.

Philip F. Esler, Ecclesiology 17 (2021) · on 2 Corinthians 2:6

This scenario takes us straight into the dynamics of the political ἐκκλησία as a voting assembly on contested issues in the Greek cities of the East.

William Tyndale, An Answer to Sir Thomas More’s Dialogue (1531) · Parker Society ed. (1850), pp. 12–14

…therefore in the translation of the new Testament, where I found this word ecclesia, I interpreted it by this word congregation.

Alexander Carson, quoted by Alexander Campbell, The Christian Baptist, July 5, 1824

Ekklesia literally signifies an assembly called out from others, and is used among the Greeks, particularly the Athenians, for their popular assemblies summoned by their chief magistrate, and in which none but citizens had a right to sit.

Alexander Campbell, The Christian System (1839), ch. XXV “The Christian Ministry,” §XIX (1871 printing, p. 84)

The community, the church, the multitude of the faithful, are the fountain of official power. This power descends from the body itself — not from its servants. Servants made by servants are servants of servants… How much surer and purer is ecclesiastic authority thus derived from Christ the head, immediately through his body, than when derived through a long, doubtful, corrupt dynasty of bishops or pontiffs!

Alexander Campbell, The Christian System (1839), ch. XXVI “The Christian Discipline,” §X (p. 88)

No wonder that broils and heart-burnings, and scandals of all sorts disturb those communities ruled by a democracy of the whole — where every thing is to be judged in public and full assembly. Such is not the Christian system.

A. D. Lindsay, The Modern Democratic State, vol. 1 (1943), p. 240 · a political philosopher
