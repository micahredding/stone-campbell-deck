# Prologue — Overview: Our Wild Democracy

[← All chapters](.)

**Slides:** [Prologue deck](decks/week0-overview.html) · **Visuals:** [Timeline](decks/elements/timeline.html) · [Theme Map](decks/elements/theme-map.html) · [Wild Democracy Grid](decks/elements/grid.html)

**The Story:** This is a class about a table — and about what a group of people on the American frontier were willing to tear down in order to keep anyone from standing in front of it.

**The Idea**: The Lord's Supper is the age to come, arriving early. Nearly everything distinctive about the Churches of Christ follows from a refusal to let anything stand between people and that future.

---

If you know the Churches of Christ at all, you probably know us by our absences. No instruments. No bishops. No creed. No denominational headquarters, no annual convention, nobody in a collar. Even the name on the sign is a refusal — not a founder's name, not a doctrine's name, just whose church it is.

Absences make a poor advertisement. Listed out like that, a tradition sounds like a subtraction problem: here is ordinary Christianity, now start taking things away. Plenty of people, inside this building and outside it, have experienced it exactly that way.

This class is going to argue something different. Absences have shapes. And if you look carefully at the shape of ours — at what is missing, and at what is conspicuously *not* missing — you find that these are not the marks of a church that wanted less. They are the negative space around something the people who built this tradition were determined to protect.

What they were protecting was a meal.

**Start with a scene we will spend all of Chapter 1 on.** August of 1801, Bourbon County, Kentucky. The roads are clogged for miles — wagons, carriages, horsemen, people on foot. Somewhere between ten and thirty thousand people, in a state that did not have many more than that to spare, converging on a log meetinghouse built to hold four hundred. It is the largest gathering anyone in America has ever seen, and what they have all come through the mud to reach is the Lord's Supper.

Why would anyone do that? What did they think was on that table?

Alexander Campbell answered it at Brush Run's first Supper in 1811. As Robert Richardson reports the sermon, the gathered church was "an emblem of the millennial state of the Church": communion is a taste of the church to come. Not a memorial. Not a ritual. Not a tender symbol of fellowship. The age to come, arriving early — in your mouth, on a Sunday morning, in a muddy field in Kentucky.

Hold onto that, and everything that follows stops looking like subtraction and starts looking like consequence.

**If the table is God's future arriving, then no one may be fenced out of it.** You cannot put a gate in front of the age to come. So at Cane Ridge, when some of the Presbyterians balked at communing with Methodists and a doctrinal test was raised at the door of the table, the test was withdrawn and the table opened. Eight hundred to eleven hundred people ate, in an age when that simply was not done.

**And if the authority structures of the church insist on the gate, then the structures have to go.** That is not a metaphor and it is not a slogan. Thomas Campbell was brought up on charges — tried, censured — for offering communion to the wrong Presbyterians. Our movement's founding rupture was *caused by a table*. Everything people find most radical about us begins as the wreckage of that collision.

**And once no one may be kept from the table, an unavoidable second question arrives: who is allowed to stand at its head?** In the Catholic tradition that question is the definition of priesthood itself — the priest is the one who may preside, and the layman is the one who may not. This movement answered it the same way it had answered the first question. Every believer a priest, and it meant the word exactly: a farmer, a teenager, someone who fixes cars all week, presiding at the Supper on Sunday as a matter of unremarkable routine. Nowhere else in Protestantism is that doctrine cashed so literally.

**A church that tore out its offices had to be held together by something else.** It was held together by things done, and done constantly — because an office persists between meetings and a practice does not. That is why the second half of this class is about practices: baptism, singing, studying, governing. Each is an act that cannot be done on your behalf, performed where everyone can see. You cannot sing without hearing that everyone around you has a voice, and without knowing they hear yours. That mutual, public, repeated knowledge is what this tradition has instead of a hierarchy.

**And then the hardest part of the story, which we will not skip.** The foretaste faded, and the practices stayed. A church that had been keeping the future's seat warm became a church keeping rules — and when the practices are the only thing holding a body together, every dispute about them becomes a fight for the body's life. That is the honest account of our worst century, and it is more sympathetic than the usual one: not pettiness, but a body defending the only skeleton it had.

Which tells you what recovery looks like. Not abandoning the practices. Remembering what they were for.

**So: "our wild democracy."** The wildness is real, and this class is not going to soften it. But notice that it is not the point — it is the price. These people did not set out to build a radically decentralized church and then decorate it with a meal. They set out to protect a meal, and a radically decentralized church is what it cost them. This is not a tradition with no center. It is a tradition that refused to let anything stand between you and its center.

Over twelve chapters we are going to walk that story from Cane Ridge forward, and at the end of each one ask the same two questions.

## Ideas

1. **We are known by our absences, and absences have shapes.** The instruments, bishops, creeds, and headquarters that are missing are not a subtraction from Christianity; they are the negative space around something being protected.
2. **The table is eschatological.** "An emblem of the millennial state of the Church" (Richardson's report of Campbell at Brush Run, 1811) — a taste of the age to come, arriving early. This is the engine of the entire story, and every other move in the class is downstream of it.
3. **Therefore no one may be fenced from it** — the open table, and the withdrawn test at Cane Ridge.
4. **Therefore the authority that fences it has to go** — Thomas Campbell's trial. The open table *causes* the founding rupture; it does not merely accompany it.
5. **Therefore no caste may stand at its head** — the priesthood of all believers, meant in the precise sense the word carries at an altar.
6. **Therefore it must be practiced, constantly, in front of each other** — the practices are the binding a church has when it has no offices.
7. **And when the foretaste is forgotten, the practices become law** — the honest account of the fracture, and the reason recovery means restarting the engine rather than discarding the machine.

## Future

*What is beautiful here? What do we carry forward?*

**The class in one paragraph — what they thought they were doing:**

They thought the future had started. Standing in the mud at Cane Ridge, watching a divided church eat at one table, they became convinced that what they were seeing was not a revival but an arrival — the first light of the age to come, breaking into Kentucky. Alexander Campbell would say it plainly at Brush Run's first Supper: in Richardson's report, the church at the table was "an emblem of the millennial state of the Church." And if that was true, then everything else followed with terrible simplicity. They were not founding a church; they thought they were ending the era of churches — the last generation of a divided Christendom, called to clear the road for what God was already bringing. So they went at the roadblocks with both hands. Creeds, hierarchies, denominational walls, the whole apparatus that decided who could come to the table and who could stand at its head — all of it, to them, was scaffolding left standing after the building had arrived, and they tore it down not out of rebellion but out of punctuality. They handed the Supper to farmers, taught plowboys to read Greek and sing in four parts, let congregations govern themselves — not because they were democrats, but because in the age they believed was dawning, all of God's people are priests, and they saw no reason to keep anyone waiting. They were wrong about the schedule. The kingdom did not come the way they expected, and their children were left holding practices whose purpose grew harder to remember. But before we decide what that means, it is worth standing where they stood and feeling how it looked from there: a table set, a door open, a future certain. The question they hand us is not whether they were right about the calendar. It is whether they were right about the table.
