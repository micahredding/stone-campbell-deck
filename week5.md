# Chapter 5 — Alexander Campbell and the Sea

**The Story:** The third instigator. Alexander Campbell, twenty years old, survives a shipwreck and vows his life to his father's ministry; in Glasgow he meets Greville Ewing and the Haldanes, lay preachers his church shut out, and gives back his communion token rather than be welcomed at a table that excluded them. In America he finds his father has reached the same place. The fledgling unity movement seeks to join the local Presbyterians and is rebuffed. Alexander answers: no human standard may close the gates of the church. The Society becomes a church, reluctantly, at Brush Run, and its first Supper is "an emblem of the millennial state of the Church." (Chapter 6: they must now ordain their own ministers.)

**The Idea**: You cannot be included by a table that excludes. The gates of the church are as wide as the gates of heaven.

## Narrative (taught 2026-11-01)

We have met two of the three instigators of our movement. Barton Stone: "Let the unity of Christians be our polar star." Thomas Campbell: "With you all we desire to unite in the bonds of an entire Christian unity." Tonight we meet the third, and he begins at sea.

On September 20, 1808, the Campbell family set out from Rich Hill, County Armagh, for Londonderry: Jane Campbell, her children, and the eldest son, Alexander, twenty years old. Thomas had sailed for America the year before and had sent for them. They waited eight days for their ship, the *Hibernia*, sailed on October 1 with her ten cannon firing in farewell, met contrary winds off Malin Head, ran before a gale all night, and anchored in Loch Indaal, on the island of Islay, to wait for wind.

On the evening of October 7, Alexander woke from a dream that the ship had struck a rock and the water was rushing in. He told the family to make ready. "I will not undress to-night," he said. "I will lay my shoes within my reach, and be ready to rise at a moment's warning."

That night a gale blew in. About ten o'clock the ship dragged her anchors onto a sunken rock; the water came in; they cut away the masts. No help could reach them until morning. Alexander sat on the stump of the broken mast, expecting to die, and thought of his father. Robert Richardson, his biographer: "He thought of his father's noble life, devoted to God and to the salvation of his fellow-beings, and felt that such a calling… was, indeed, the highest and most worthy sphere of action in which any human being could engage." And then: "It was then in that solemn hour, that he gave himself up wholly to God, and resolved that, if saved from the present peril, he would certainly spend his entire life in the ministry of the gospel."

Everyone survived. And what Alexander did not know was that, in Pennsylvania, his father had been tried, censured and suspended by the Seceders, and a week before the family set out had broken with them. The son had just vowed his life to his father's calling. In Glasgow, where the family wintered for ten months, he would embrace, one by one, the three things his father was tried for.

**He went to hear other preachers.** Above all Greville Ewing, who preached to fifteen hundred people in a converted circus. That was "occasional hearing," the very thing his father had been condemned for. With his father an ocean away, Richardson says, Alexander could think for himself.

**He honored laymen who preached.** At Ewing's house he heard the story of Robert and James Haldane: two brothers, sea captains turned laymen, who gave their fortune and their lives to the gospel. When the Church of Scotland would not send the gospel abroad, because there was plenty of unbelief at home, the Haldanes preached at home: in streets, barns and fields, "to two or two hundred." The church's answer was hostility. "The clergy… soon began to manifest their hostility to lay-preaching; and the parish minister took means to deprive them of the house in which the meetings were held." In Kintyre in 1800 they were held under arrest by the Highland chiefs, "at the instigation of the clergy." Meanwhile a prominent minister of the same church had published a book denying the divinity and the atonement of Christ, and kept his pulpit. Laymen preaching Christ: arrested. A minister denying him: kept his pulpit. What Alexander saw, Richardson says, was "the disposition they constantly manifested to exercise the power which they possessed in an arbitrary manner."

But it was his father's church, and "he did not feel himself at liberty to abandon rashly the cherished religious sentiments of his youth."

**And then the table.** In the spring of 1809 the communion season came. He was examined and given the token, the small lead ticket that admitted a member to the table. He was welcome at his church's table. Ewing was not. The Haldanes were not. Can you be included by a church that excludes?

Richardson describes the struggle: "His conscientious misgivings as to the propriety of sanctioning any longer, by participation, a religious system which he disapproved, and, on the other hand, his sincere desire to comply with all his religious obligations, created a serious conflict in his mind, from which he found it impossible to escape." There were eight hundred communicants that day, at eight or nine tables. He waited for the last one, still hoping to overcome his scruples. When the plate came round he dropped his token on it and declined the bread and wine: "the ring of the token, falling upon the plate, announced the instant at which he renounced Presbyterianism for ever — the leaden voucher becoming thus a token not of communion but of separation." He told no one. Welcomed at a table that turned others away, he gave back the token.

In August 1809 the family sailed again, on the *Latonia*: fifty-one days at sea, and a squall on August 27 that nearly sank her. They landed at New York on September 29 and set out by wagon from Philadelphia for Washington, Pennsylvania. Thomas rode out to meet them, and on the road he told his son everything: the trial, the rebuke, and that he had left the Seceders. Alexander had carried his own decision alone for six months and expected his father to oppose him. Instead, Richardson says, "he found him already, though by a somewhat different method, led practically to the very same conclusions." The father had opened the table to people who had no token. The son had held a valid token and would not use it. Neither knew.

Alexander took up his father's cause. Thomas had just written the *Declaration and Address* for the Christian Association of Washington, and Alexander read it in proof. But a society is not a church: its members "were deprived of various privileges which belonged to the church relation." No Lord's Supper, no baptism, no ordination. The Society "must obtain admission into some regularly organized religious body, or be itself compelled to change its attitude and resolve itself into an independent Church — an alternative which Thomas Campbell particularly desired to avoid."

So the Campbells sought out Presbyterian communion. In October 1810 Thomas asked the Synod of Pittsburg to receive him and the Association. They were rebuffed.

On November 1, 1810, Alexander answered, before the Association. On baptism: "We dare not inculcate infant baptism in the name of the Lord as indispensably incumbent upon Christians, because the Lord has nowhere expressly enjoined it… we think it highly anti-scriptural to make it a term of communion, for to do this is to make it a term of salvation." To make it a term of communion is to say, "You are excluded from the Church below, consequently from the Church above"; it is to make it a term of salvation "as much at least as the Judaizing teachers did their beloved circumcision." It would "reject a great number of God's dear children," against the command, "Receive ye one another as Christ has also received us." It should be "a matter of forbearance… by no means considering it a matter of indifference." And: "We declare that neither paedobaptism nor antipaedobaptism availeth anything." He was not rejecting the practice of baptism; he was rejecting it as a fence.

On creeds as terms of communion: "Standards had been lifted up which narrowed the gates of Zion… and there were none of them but would reject those whom God has not rejected, and deny admission to those whom God had admitted." Under those standards, "the apostle Paul, the angel Gabriel in human form, could not be admitted." That same year he said why he could join no party: "Because no party will receive into communion all whom God would receive into heaven." And so: "We have decided, therefore,… to open the gates of admission into the Church as wide as the gates of heaven."

The Synod's answer had already come: "Mr. Campbell's request to be received into… Christian communion cannot be granted."

The refusal did not end their search for a larger union. But to meet the spiritual needs of their people, Thomas, very reluctantly, agreed to constitute a church. On Saturday, May 4, 1811, at the Brush Run meeting house, about thirty members formed a church. Thomas was appointed elder, Alexander was licensed to preach, and four deacons were chosen. They sang Psalm 118 in the old Scottish metrical version. "It was with great reluctance that he finally concluded to take this step."

The next day, May 5, the new church kept the Lord's Supper for the first time. Alexander preached on "I am that bread of life," and he told them what they were doing: "in assembling there on that occasion, they furnished an emblem of the millennial state of the Church, nay, an emblem of the heavenly state, when men of different nations, and known by different names, should sit down together in the kingdom of God."

Three instigators, one table. Barton Stone in 1803 withdrew from the Synod's "jurisdiction, but not from their communion." Thomas Campbell in 1807 was tried for serving the Supper to Presbyterians outside the Seceders. Alexander Campbell in 1809 gave back the token. Three men from three countries, none knowing the others' story, each broken with his church over who may come to the table.

## Ideas

1. **You cannot be included by a table that excludes.** The token. He was welcome at his church's table; Ewing and the Haldanes were not. To take the bread would have been "sanctioning… by participation" a system that shut them out, so he gave the token back.
2. **No one may be required to do what God has not required.** Infant baptism: "the Lord has nowhere expressly enjoined it"; to make it a term of communion "is to make it a term of salvation." Creeds: standards that "reject those whom God has not rejected." The *Declaration and Address*, in Alexander's own words.
3. **Neither side of a disputed question is the gospel.** "Neither paedobaptism nor antipaedobaptism availeth anything." He cared about baptism ("by no means… a matter of indifference") and still would not make either side a fence. Forbearance, not indifference.
4. **The gates of the church as wide as the gates of heaven.** "To open the gates of admission into the Church as wide as the gates of heaven." The Synod would not stand on that ground; Stone, Thomas and Alexander each did.
5. **The table is a foretaste of the church to come.** The first Supper at Brush Run: "an emblem of the millennial state of the Church." Not only a memory of the cross; a taste of the future.
