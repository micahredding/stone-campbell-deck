# Chapter 7 — Millennial Harbinger

[← All chapters](.)

**The Story:** In 1830, Alexander Campbell pivoted from attack journalism, to building for the age to come. Millennial Harbinger became the leading journal of the movement, with its vision of societal and religious reform, science and education, and eschatology as north star.

**The Idea**: Our movement's animating impulse was eschatology. Not looking backwards, but looking forwards to embody the age to come. Not just for the church, but for the world. We continue the same impulse today—NT Wright, Josh Graves telling us to live as a microcosm of the future. That's not a new discovery, it's one of our deepest impulses.

1. We live as a microcosm of the age to come
2. Eschatology is our tradition
3. Eschatology drives us in various ways, which may look very different
4. The church's life now is a taste of God's future
5. Its unity is founded on Christ alone, so it needs freedom of conscience
6. Our practices are ways of living that future now

## Narrative (draft, in Micah's order, 2026-11-08)

### 1. The pivot: from demolition to destination

For seven years (1823–1830) the *Christian Baptist* tore things down: the clergy, creeds, the "kingdom of the clergy." In January 1830 Campbell started a new paper and, by July, closed the old one. He named the new one for the future.

> "THIS work shall be devoted to the destruction of Sectarianism, Infidelity, and Antichristian doctrine and practice. It shall have for its object the developement, and introduction of that political and religious order of society called THE MILLENNIUM, which will be the consummation of that ultimate amelioration of society proposed in the Christian Scriptures."
> (Prospectus, *Millennial Harbinger* 1, no. 1, January 4, 1830, p. 1)

The rest of the Prospectus is a list of real work in the real world:
- "The incompatibility of any sectarian establishment, now known on earth, with the genius of the glorious age to come."
- "The inadequacy of all the present systems of education… to delvelope [sic] the powers of the human mind." (Bacon College, 1836; Bethany College, 1840)
- "The *injustice* which yet remains in many of the political regulations under the best political governments, when contrasted with the *justice* which Christianity proposes."
- "Disquisitions upon the treatment of African slaves, as preparatory to their emancipation." (Gradual, and he never made slaveholding a test of fellowship. Say so, briefly; Chapter 9 picks this up.)

The freedom of the early chapters was *for* something. The Millennium is "a political and religious order of society": not an escape from the world but this world, set right. And it begins in the church: "We expect no new Sun, no new revelation of the Spirit, no other than the same gospel and the same religion, only that it shall be disinterred from the rubbish of the dark ages" (Prefatory Remarks, 1830).

### 2. Our problem with Campbell

Reading Campbell is conflicted for us. The tradition built on him a "pattern theology": the New Testament as a blueprint, read by command, example and necessary inference, with every practice a test of faithfulness. We have rejected that hermeneutic. So what, if anything, can we keep from his outlook?

Two facts help:
- "Thus saith the Lord" began as a shield against legalism, not a blueprint. Thomas Campbell used it to limit what anyone could *impose*: no one may "inculcate anything of human authority, of private opinion, or inventions of men… or anything as matter of Christian faith or duty, for which there can not be expressly produced a 'Thus saith the Lord, either in express terms, or by approved precedent'" (D&A, Article V). It is a test that strikes requirements out, not one that builds a positive pattern to be matched.
  - Where Scripture is silent, "no human authority has power to interfere, in order to supply the supposed deficiency by making laws for the Church; nor can anything more be required of Christians in such cases, but only that they observe these commands and ordinances as will evidently answer the declared and obvious end of their institution." (Prop. 5) Note the measure: the *purpose* of a practice, not its exact form.
  - Inferences "are… not formally binding upon the consciences of Christians farther than they perceive the connection… Therefore, no such deductions can be made terms of communion." (Prop. 6)
  - The later hermeneutic turned this around: command, example *and* necessary inference became a positive pattern to be reproduced, and binding inference (the very thing Prop. 6 made non-binding) became standard a generation later, with Lard and McGarvey (Jorgenson, p. 325 n. 2).
- Campbell himself didn't treat restoration as an end in itself. It pointed somewhere (section 4).

### 3. An observation: we are Wright-pilled

Have you noticed? Otter Creek, and increasingly Churches of Christ generally, read and quote N. T. Wright. [Micah: examples from Otter Creek: Josh Graves, the sermons, classes, books on the shelf.] Why are we drawn so strongly to what he is doing?

### 4. The proposal: Wright is doing today what Campbell was doing 200 years ago

**(1) A historical reading of the Bible: story, not propositions.**
- Campbell started from the best textual scholarship of his day (the *Living Oracles*, 1826, built on Griesbach's Greek text) and read the Bible as history: "The Bible is a book of facts, not of opinions, theories, abstract generalities, nor of verbal definitions… The meaning of the Bible facts is the true biblical doctrine. History is therefore the plan pursued in both Testaments… history and prophecy, make up exactly four-fifths of all the volumes of inspiration." (*Christian System*, p. 19)
- Wright, a historian of the first century, reads it as a story we are invited into: "the authority of the Bible is the authority of a love story in which we are invited to take part… a novel in which… there is still some way to go, and we are invited to become living, participating, intelligent, and decision-making characters within the story." (*Simply Christian*, p. 186)
- So the question becomes where we are in the story and how to tell it, not which propositions to sign, or how to solve the logical puzzle of the atonement.
- What Campbell means by "facts": **things done**, that is, history. "Fact means something done. The term deed, so common in the reign of James I. is equivalent to our term fact… That God exists, is a truth, but not a fact; that he created the heavens and the earth, is a fact and a truth… something must be done or effected before we have a fact." (*Christian System*, pp. 115–116) "By facts we always mean something said or done. The works of God and the words of God, or the things done or spoken by God, are those facts which are laid down and exhibited in the Bible as the foundation of all faith, hope, love, piety, and humanity." (p. 123)
  - So "a book of facts" is not a rulebook of data points; it is a book of God's deeds. Campbell sets facts *against* propositions ("not of opinions, theories, abstract generalities"), which is Wright's move too: Scripture as the story of what God has done, and is doing.
  - And Campbell's facts reach forward: "Moses begins with asserting facts that had transpired in creation and providence; and John ends with asserting prophetic or prospective facts in the future displays of providence and redemption. Facts, then, are the alpha and the omega." (p. 115) The story has a future, which leads straight into (2).
  - A smaller note, for the notes only: his vocabulary is Baconian ("all true science is acquired from the observation and comparison of facts," p. 123). The later "book of facts as blueprint" reading, which Richardson criticized in the 1850s, flattened "deeds" into data.

**(2) Eschatology as the driving category: the world renewed.**
- Campbell: the Millennium as "that political and religious order of society… the consummation of that ultimate amelioration of society proposed in the Christian Scriptures" (Prospectus, dated 1829, printed Jan. 1830).
- Wright: "The life of the church is supposed to be a forward pointer to the long-promised cosmic reality." (*God's Homecoming*, p. 220)
- **The future is practical (use this, Micah 2026-11-09):** "This is not, indeed, a speculative and uninteresting subject. It is highly practical… It is therefore a practical question." Those who expect the millennium "are full of ways and means for the diffusion of knowledge, for the intellectual and moral improvement of society." (A. C., "The Coming of the Lord—No. I," *MH* Jan. 1841, pp. 10–11) Bethany College opened that November.
- Renewal, not escape: "God recreates, regenerates, but annihilates nothing." (*Christian System*, p. 311) The new earth is "the everlasting home of man; for the tabernacle of God is to be there forever." (*MH* Feb. 1841, p. 54)
- **The point is the future itself, not the program (Micah 2026-11-09).** The leaders could not agree on the schedule, and changed their minds:
  - Campbell, 1830: confident ("ultimate amelioration of society"). 1831: "a deepening gloom overspreads our ecclesiastical heavens." 1833: "For the last five or six years we have been waiting for fair winds and a serene sky"; no "radical amelioration of society" till "Jesus appears in the clouds of heaven." *Christian System*: leaves open "how far by moral, and how far by physical means."
  - Walter Scott: gradual in 1826; "the Millennium will commence in one day" (c. 1830–42); by 1859, "It is not an age to drop down from heaven, but a period of light, religion and enjoyment to arise out of the natural and gradual progress of society." (*The Messiahship*, p. 335)
  - Stone: Christ comes first and reigns (from 1832–33), but "Our wisdom is to be always ready" (*CM* 1834, p. 148).
  - So the significance was never the details of the eschatological program. It was the future itself: a future that defines the church now.
- Keep at hand for his social program (not necessarily this week): the Cherokees. "…this most important community of the aborigines, to whom God gave this continent… if none but the eyes of God were upon some of them, I think they would send these poor defenceless Indians beyond the Rocky Mountains, if it would not cost too much." (*MH* Jan. 1830, pp. 44–45; he attacks the Baptist *Columbian Star* for supporting removal "in defiance of all treaties," June 1830, p. 258.) This is the Prospectus's "injustice… contrasted with the justice… which the millennial order of society promises," applied. Shadow beside it: "Even in the Millennium, I presume there will be masters and servants" (*MH* Mar. 1830, p. 129).
- Caution (notes): Campbell's millennium is earthly history before Christ returns, with the new creation after; Wright's renewal comes by God's act at the resurrection. An analogy, not an identity.

**(3) The church as a small working model of new creation, and its substance is unity.**
- Wright: "…to think of the vocation of the church as the call to live as the small working model of new creation." (*The Vision of Ephesians*, §1) "…because in the future life we will be… united across all ethnic and other differences, it is all the more important that we anticipate this as far as possible in the present." (*God's Homecoming*, p. 273)
- Brush Run's first Supper, May 5, 1811: "in assembling there on that occasion, they furnished an emblem of the millennial state of the Church, nay, an emblem of the heavenly state, when men of different nations, and known by different names, should sit down together in the kingdom of God." (Richardson's report of Alexander's sermon, *Memoirs* I:369. Credit Richardson.)
- Campbell, 1830: "To see even a few scores of intelligent christians… meeting around one and the same Lord's table, and uniting in the praises and adorations of one and the same common Lord and Saviour, imparts to us a joy which we are unable to express." (Prefatory Remarks)
- Campbell goes further than Wright: unity now is not only a foretaste but the cause. "…to suppose that all christians will form one communion in that happy age of the world, and not before it, is to suppose a moral effect without a cause." (*Christian System*, p. 113) "…the union of christians is essential to the conversion of the world." (same page)
- Wright: "Paul would be shocked by the very idea of 'denominations', sitting close to one another on the same street but not even trying to join up to share in mission and resources." (*VE* §6)
- **Unity founded on Christ alone is the eschatological unity (Micah 2026-11-09).** If the church's unity rests on anything other than Christ (a creed, a party, a sword, a state), it is not the real unity; founded on Christ alone, it is the unity of the age to come. So freedom of conscience is an eschatological prerequisite, not a concession.
  - Thomas Campbell, 1809 (use): "What! shall we pray for a thing, and not strive to obtain it!!" (*D&A*)
  - Alexander Campbell, 1828 (use): "When then we see a people making new divisions and keeping up old ones, while praying for the Millennium or the triumph of love and harmony, we as naturally suspect that they are any thing but in earnest." It is like "a nation preparing for war, and praying for peace." (*Christian Baptist* 5, p. 263)
  - Freedom of opinion as the way there (here, or in the gradualism side path): "WILL sects ever cease?… How then shall it be introduced? I answer unequivocally in one sentence, By abandoning opinions, and founding all associations upon the belief of gospel facts… We do not ask them to give up their opinions—We ask them only not to impose them upon others." (*MH* Apr. 1830, pp. 145–146)
  - Not by the sword: "No kingdom now on earth can be regarded as a kingdom of our Lord, because they were all founded by the sword." (*MH* 1833, p. 121) "To conquer an enemy is to convert him into a friend." (*MH* July 1830, p. 304) A church established by law is "an impudent imposition or base counterfeit… When Christianity gains the throne, Jesus Christ will place it there himself." (*MH* 1833, p. 120)
- So we ask: what does it look like for us to model new creation, and its unity, in the life and practice of the church?

### 5. The surprise: a context in which "pattern" makes sense

**The substructure (Micah 2026-11-09).** Sunday worship is a taste of the future: at the table, in worship, everyone is a citizen king and priest to God. The "pattern" arose out of the question of how to live the future in the present.
- "[He] has made all the citizens of his kingdom, not citizens only, but citizen kings and priests to God." (July 4th oration, *MH* 1830, p. 309)
- "Being a royal priesthood… they have a right to meet in his name to break the loaf of blessing, to drink the cup of blessing, without the intervention of a mediator, priest, or prophet, consecrated by human hands." (*MH* Aug. 1830, p. 368)
- **New (round 2): the Lord's Day as foretaste.** "The Lord's day not only commemorates the resurrection of Jesus, but anticipates the morning of the resurrection." (*MH* Jan. 1834, p. 37) The day is "the beginning of a new creation"; the first Christians kept it with the loaf and cup, "songs of praise," "addresses of every sort," prayers "for all mankind," and "donations for the poor," "a day of pure, holy, and celestial joy—a day of social bliss." And against the one-man service: ministers "who enjoin silence upon all, that they alone may be heard… have yet to learn the New Testament religion." ("Reformation—No. XII. The Lord's Day," *MH* Sept. 1836, pp. 418–421)
- The church at worship: "the only holy place now on earth—the Christian church," its table set for "a royal priesthood" (*CS* pp. 322–323); churches "like so many candlesticks in a large edifice… designed to enlighten and convert the world" (*CS*, from Extra No. VII, 1834); "the king and the beggar meet on one platform" (*MH* 1853).
- The pattern was not a liturgy: "an economy of principles" (*CB* 1830); a fixed order would be "a liturgy, a ritual form like the Jewish" (*MH* June 1838).
- The table as foretaste: Brush Run's "emblem of the millennial state" (Richardson); Campbell's joy at Christians "meeting around one and the same Lord's table" (1830); Walter Scott: the Supper and the Lord's Day are "pillars, monumental pillars… whose base and summit connect together the first and second coming of their author; for his command is to 'do this' until he comes again" (*The Gospel Restored*, 1836, p. 398).

*Caution (round 2):* in 1834 Campbell expected no general restoration of the ancient order before "the great and notable day of the Lord"; by 1840 he is back to the 1825 view: "all the Millennium we could scripturally expect was not merely the restoration of the Jerusalem church… but the extension of it through all nations and languages for one thousand years" (*MH* Dec. 1840, p. 561). Use 1840 beside 1825, and don't present 1825 as unbroken.

Campbell, 1825: "Just in so far as the ancient order of things, or the religion of the New Testament, is restored, just so far has the Millennium commenced, and so far has its blessings been enjoyed." (*Christian Baptist* 2, Feb. 7, 1825, p. 136)

And in the *Harbinger*'s first issue he makes the future the test of doctrine: "In detecting the false gospels, nothing will aid us so much as an examination of their tendencies, and a comparison of their effects with what the Millennium proposes. The gospel of no sect can convert the world." (Prefatory Remarks, *Millennial Harbinger* 1, no. 1, 1830) A doctrine is judged by where it leads: does it produce the united, converted world the Millennium promises?

For Campbell, restoring the ancient order was not legalism. It was the way the future begins. Read that way, the question behind our practices is not "what are we permitted?" but "how do we best testify to the future that is coming?"
- How do we best visibly embody the unity of the church? (One loaf, an open table, "unity of faith, for unity of opinion.")
- How do we best visibly embody the priesthood of all believers? (Any member at the table; the congregation ordains; everyone sings.)
- How do we best visibly embody the future that is coming?

Campbell's own summary of the program, 1835: "Let the bible be substituted for all human creeds; facts, for definitions; things, for words; faith, for speculation; unity of faith, for unity of opinion; the positive commandments of God, for human legislation and tradition; piety, for ceremony; morality, for partizan zeal; the practice of religion, for the mere profession of it: and the work is done." (*Christian System*, p. 115)

This sets up Chapters 10–13: baptism, singing, Scripture and governance as ways of embodying the coming future, not as tests.
