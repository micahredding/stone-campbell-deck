# Chapter 7 — Millennial Harbinger

[← All chapters](.)

**The Story:** In 1830, Alexander Campbell pivoted from attack journalism, to building for the age to come. Millennial Harbinger became the leading journal of the movement, with its vision of societal and religious reform, science and education, and eschatology as north star.

**The Idea**: Our movement's animating impulse was eschatology. Not looking backwards, but looking forwards to embody the age to come. Not just for the church, but for the world. We continue the same impulse today—NT Wright, Josh Graves telling us to live as a microcosm of the future. That's not a new discovery, it's one of our deepest impulses.

## Narrative (draft, in Micah's order, 2026-11-08)

### 1. The pivot: from demolition to destination

For seven years (1823–1830) the *Christian Baptist* tore things down: the clergy, creeds, the "kingdom of the clergy." In January 1830 Campbell started a new paper and, by July, closed the old one. He named the new one for the future.

> "THIS work shall be devoted to the destruction of Sectarianism, Infidelity, and Antichristian doctrine and practice. It shall have for its object the developement, and introduction of that political and religious order of society called THE MILLENNIUM, which will be the consummation of that ultimate amelioration of society proposed in the Christian Scriptures."
> (Prospectus, *Millennial Harbinger* 1, no. 1, January 4, 1830, p. 1)

The rest of the Prospectus is a list of real work in the real world:
- "The incompatibility of any sectarian establishment, now known on earth, with the genius of the glorious age to come."
- "The inadequacy of all the present systems of education… to delvelope [sic] the powers of the human mind." (Bacon College, 1836; Bethany College, 1840)
- "The *injustice* which yet remains in many of the political regulations under the best political governments, when contrasted with the *justice* which Christianity proposes."
- "Disquisitions upon the treatment of African slaves, as preparatory to their emancipation." (Gradual, and he never made slaveholding a test of fellowship. Say so, briefly; Chapter 9 picks this up.)

The freedom of the early chapters was *for* something. The Millennium is "a political and religious order of society": not an escape from the world but this world, set right. And it begins in the church: "We expect no new Sun, no new revelation of the Spirit, no other than the same gospel and the same religion, only that it shall be disinterred from the rubbish of the dark ages" (Prefatory Remarks, 1830).

### 2. Our problem with Campbell

Reading Campbell is conflicted for us. The tradition built on him a "pattern theology": the New Testament as a blueprint, read by command, example and necessary inference, with every practice a test of faithfulness. We have rejected that hermeneutic. So what, if anything, can we keep from his outlook?

Two facts help:
- The pattern was narrower at the start. Thomas Campbell's test was "Thus saith the Lord, either in express terms, or by approved precedent" (D&A), and he made inferences "not formally binding upon the consciences of Christians" (Prop. 6). Binding "necessary inference" became standard a generation later, with Lard and McGarvey (Jorgenson, p. 325 n. 2).
- Campbell himself didn't treat restoration as an end in itself. It pointed somewhere (section 4).

### 3. An observation: we are Wright-pilled

Have you noticed? Otter Creek, and increasingly Churches of Christ generally, read and quote N. T. Wright. [Micah: examples from Otter Creek: Josh Graves, the sermons, classes, books on the shelf.] Why are we drawn so strongly to what he is doing?

### 4. The proposal: Wright is doing today what Campbell was doing 200 years ago

**(1) A historical reading of the Bible: story, not propositions.**
- Campbell started from the best textual scholarship of his day (the *Living Oracles*, 1826, built on Griesbach's Greek text) and read the Bible as history: "The Bible is a book of facts, not of opinions, theories, abstract generalities, nor of verbal definitions… The meaning of the Bible facts is the true biblical doctrine. History is therefore the plan pursued in both Testaments… history and prophecy, make up exactly four-fifths of all the volumes of inspiration." (*Christian System*, p. 19)
- Wright, a historian of the first century, reads it as a story we are invited into: "the authority of the Bible is the authority of a love story in which we are invited to take part… a novel in which… there is still some way to go, and we are invited to become living, participating, intelligent, and decision-making characters within the story." (*Simply Christian*, p. 186)
- So the question becomes where we are in the story and how to tell it, not which propositions to sign, or how to solve the logical puzzle of the atonement.
- Caution: Campbell's "facts" are Baconian and Lockean, and in the 1850s Richardson himself criticized the "book of facts" approach. The analogy is the move from system to history, not identical method.

**(2) Eschatology as the driving category: the world renewed.**
- Campbell: the Millennium as "that political and religious order of society… the consummation of that ultimate amelioration of society proposed in the Christian Scriptures" (Prospectus).
- Wright: "The life of the church is supposed to be a forward pointer to the long-promised cosmic reality." (*God's Homecoming*, p. 220)
- Both look forward to the transformation of this world, and both let that future shape the church's work and identity now.
- Caution: Wright's new creation is God's act at the resurrection; Campbell's millennium comes gradually, through reform under providence, and he still expected heaven beyond it. An analogy, not an identity. His optimism also dimmed: uncertainty about prophecy from 1833, then the Civil War (Chapter 9).

**(3) The church as a small working model of new creation, and its substance is unity.**
- Wright: "…to think of the vocation of the church as the call to live as the small working model of new creation." (*The Vision of Ephesians*, §1) "…because in the future life we will be… united across all ethnic and other differences, it is all the more important that we anticipate this as far as possible in the present." (*God's Homecoming*, p. 273)
- Brush Run's first Supper, May 5, 1811: "in assembling there on that occasion, they furnished an emblem of the millennial state of the Church, nay, an emblem of the heavenly state, when men of different nations, and known by different names, should sit down together in the kingdom of God." (Richardson's report of Alexander's sermon, *Memoirs* I:369. Credit Richardson.)
- Campbell, 1830: "To see even a few scores of intelligent christians… meeting around one and the same Lord's table, and uniting in the praises and adorations of one and the same common Lord and Saviour, imparts to us a joy which we are unable to express." (Prefatory Remarks)
- Campbell goes further than Wright: unity now is not only a foretaste but the cause. "…to suppose that all christians will form one communion in that happy age of the world, and not before it, is to suppose a moral effect without a cause." (*Christian System*, p. 113) "…the union of christians is essential to the conversion of the world." (same page)
- Wright: "Paul would be shocked by the very idea of 'denominations', sitting close to one another on the same street but not even trying to join up to share in mission and resources." (*VE* §6)
- So we ask: what does it look like for us to model new creation, and its unity, in the life and practice of the church?

### 5. The surprise: a context in which "pattern" makes sense

Campbell, 1825: "Just in so far as the ancient order of things, or the religion of the New Testament, is restored, just so far has the Millennium commenced, and so far has its blessings been enjoyed." (*Christian Baptist* 2, Feb. 7, 1825, p. 136)

For Campbell, restoring the ancient order was not legalism. It was the way the future begins. Read that way, the question behind our practices is not "what are we permitted?" but "how do we best testify to the future that is coming?"
- How do we best visibly embody the unity of the church? (One loaf, an open table, "unity of faith, for unity of opinion.")
- How do we best visibly embody the priesthood of all believers? (Any member at the table; the congregation ordains; everyone sings.)
- How do we best visibly embody the future that is coming?

Campbell's own summary of the program, 1835: "Let the bible be substituted for all human creeds; facts, for definitions; things, for words; faith, for speculation; unity of faith, for unity of opinion; the positive commandments of God, for human legislation and tradition; piety, for ceremony; morality, for partizan zeal; the practice of religion, for the mere profession of it: and the work is done." (*Christian System*, p. 115)

This sets up Chapters 10–13: baptism, singing, Scripture and governance as ways of embodying the coming future, not as tests.
