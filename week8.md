# Chapter 8 — The Stone-Campbell Merger (1832)

[← All chapters](.)

**The Story:** In Lexington, two unity and reform movements, represented by Barton W Stone and Raccoon John Smith, come together to work out unity with each other. Yet after days of wrestling, they disagree on many foundational issues. The only basis for unity they can come up with is the Bible. And that's what they shake on.

**The Idea**: Our movement is united around a commitment to the Bible—NOT agreement on _interpretations_ of the Bible! While uniting on interpretations is impossible, uniting on commitment to wrestle with Scripture actually worked at least once.

**The slogans (Micah asked, 2026-11-18).**
- **"No creed but the Bible" is Stone's own phrase:**
  - "We determined that we would own no name but Christian, which we derive from our union with Christ; and that we would subscribe to no creed but the Bible" (CM 3, 1828, "Reply to Elder John Powell's Queries No. III", p. 231);
  - "You know, sir, that I owned no creed but the Bible, and that I was ordained with this understanding by the Presbytery" (CM 6, 1832, letter to J. L. Wilson);
  - at Lexington, Stone in Williams: "After we had given up all creeds and taken the Bible, and the Bible alone" (p. 454).
- **"No creed but Christ" is later.** The *Encyclopedia* ("Creeds and Confessions") traces it to Walter Scott's "golden oracle", the Petrine confession that Jesus is the Christ, "immortalized in the slogan 'No creed but Christ'". It gives no author. The full form, "No creed but Christ, no book but the Bible, no law but love, no name but the divine", is in the *Encyclopedia*'s "Slogans" entry (Terry Miethe). The earliest uses in our corpus are T. W. Phillips at the 1909 Centennial Convention and Garrison (1926). For its history: W. J. Jarman et al., "The Slogans of the Disciples," *Christian-Evangelist* (1949). Dwight Stevenson (1960s): the two slogans are "very different proposals".

**III. (b) What they disagreed on** (one slide, built in, one line per click; Williams, p. 450, read off)

**IV. (a) What was said**
9. John Smith rises first: "God has but one people on the earth. He has given to them but one Book". (p. 452)
10. Smith: "While there is but one faith, there may be ten thousand opinions; and hence, if Christians are ever to be one, they must be one in faith, and not in opinion." (p. 453)
11. Smith: "let us all come to the Bible, and to the Bible alone, as the only book in the world that can give us all the Light we need." (p. 454)
12. Stone answers: "Christians never can be one in their speculations upon those mysterious and sublime subjects". (p. 454)
13. Stone: "I have not one objection to the ground laid down by him as the true scriptural basis of union among the people of God; and I am willing to give him, now and here, my hand." (p. 455; checked on the scan.)
14. **The handshake.** Elders and teachers come forward and join hands; "A song arose". (p. 455)
15. **The loaf.** "On Lord's day, they broke the loaf together". (p. 455) Notes: Williams is the only source for the Jan. 1 communion; Smith's own letter (CM, Feb. 1832) confirms shared tables in the following weeks.

17a. **The quiz, answered** (callback to slide 2; built in):
    - "No creed but the Bible" is the founders' own phrase, Stone's: "We determined that we would own no name but Christian… and that we would subscribe to no creed but the Bible" (CM 3, 1828, p. 231). At Lexington: "After we had given up all creeds and taken the Bible, and the Bible alone" (Williams, p. 454).
    - "No creed but Christ" came later: from Scott's confession that Jesus is the Christ, as a slogan by the 1900s ("No creed but Christ, no book but the Bible, no law but love, no name but the divine").
    - *Notes:* the room divided the way the movement did. **Dwight E. Stevenson** (1906–1996), a Disciples preacher who taught preaching at the College of the Bible (Lexington Theological Seminary) from 1947 and wrote the biography *Walter Scott: Voice of the Golden Oracle* (1946), took this up for the Disciples' Panel of Scholars in "Faith versus Theology in the Thought of the Disciple Fathers," *The Renewal of Church: The Panel Reports*, vol. 2 (1963), pp. 33–60.
      - In his own words: "to state the anomaly as boldly as possible, the movement is at one and the same time Christocentric and bibliocentric." (Quoted in the *Encyclopedia of the Stone-Campbell Movement*, "Faith".)
      - "Very different proposals" is Paul M. Blowers's summary of Stevenson's argument (*Encyclopedia*, "Creeds and Confessions"), not Stevenson's own phrase: "No Creed but Christ" and "No Creed but the Bible" were "as different as allegiance to Christ and allegiance to the strict letter of the scriptural text." The point for this morning: the class gave two answers to one slogan, and still sits at one table. The same was true of Lexington.
**Walter Scott: the basis of "No creed but Christ"** (all checked against the extracted texts; OCR-only differences).
- **The year of Lexington, both slogans in one sentence.** On the primitive church: "her only manual was, 'The Bible.' And her all comprehensive creed for the binding of the faithful; 'Jesus Christ is the Son of God.' On this faith and this volume she gathered her children". (Scott, "Primitive Sufferings," *The Evangelist* 1, 1832, p. 32) The Bible is the *manual*; Christ is the *creed*. For the founders, "no creed but the Bible" and "no creed but Christ" were two sides of one thing, not rival answers.
- **The Father's oracle as the creed.** "Behold my beloved Son in whom I am well pleased; hear ye him." The Father "proposes it for the religious creed of all the world". (*The Evangelist* 1, 1832, p. 3)
- **The creed and the basis of union.** In *The Messiahship* (1859), Scott lists the "practical bearings" of the confession that Jesus is the Christ: it is "the creed", "the element of confession for the remission of sins", and "the basis of union among all Christians" (p. 273; OCR damaged, read as paraphrase).
- **Checked lines from *The Messiahship*:**
  - "It shows the true creed, and restores it to its native eminence above all other things in the gospel." (p. 272)
  - "As a creed, it is exclusive of all foreign matter, and will make no concession to, or compromises with any other creed, Catholic or Protestant." (p. 273)
  - "By a series of negations, I might exclude every creed in Christendom from its claims to be called the creed of our religion" (pp. 274–275; page to confirm)
  - "the creed, built exclusively on Christ as the Son of the living God" (p. 211)
  - In the preface, on the reformers' distinctive: "if any section of the Christian profession offers it in its practical forms as the basis of Christian union, the symbol of confession for the remission of sins, and the element of our perfection in holiness, save our brethren, the author is not aware of it." (p. 7)
- **For the quiz callback.** If the class splits between "Christ" and "the Bible", Scott (1832) says both: the Bible as the "only manual", Christ as the "all comprehensive creed". The later split (Stevenson's "Christocentric and bibliocentric") pulled apart what Scott held together.

17. John Rogers: "We solemnly pledged ourselves to one another before God, to abandon all speculations, especially on the Trinity". (Stone, *Biography*, p. 343)

**V. (c) How long it took** (a timeline slide, built in, then the Lexington episode)
18. The timeline:
    - Jan. 1832: Smith (Reformer) and Rogers (Christian) ride out together as evangelists.
    - Feb. 1832: the two Lexington congregations pledge union, break bread together, and dissolve the pledge (Feb. 25).
    - Feb. 1832: Smith has to defend himself to alarmed Reformers.
    - 1834: Jacksonville, Ill.: Stone joins neither church "until they united together".
    - July 1835: the Lexington congregations finally unite.
19. **Lexington, February 1832.** Why it broke: "no person but a preacher has a right to administer the ordinances—such as the breaking of the loaf"; "alarmed at the idea of us common folks receiving the name of kings and priests to the Lord." (H. C. C., MH 1832, p. 192)
20. **July 1835.** "Nor was a union between the two congregations finally and formally effected till in July, 1835". (Williams, p. 460)
21. **Stone, years later:** union would have come as easily elsewhere "had there not been a few ignorant, headstrong bigots on both sides". (Stone, *Biography*; page to confirm)
22. **Stone:** "This union, irrespective of reproach, I view as the noblest act of my life." (*Biography*, p. 79)

**VI. Takeaway**
23. They did not unite on agreement.
24. They united around the Bible, not on agreement about how to read it.
25. Ideas, discussion, site.
