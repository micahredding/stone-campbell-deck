# Chapter 8 — The Stone-Campbell Merger (1832)

[← All chapters](.)

**The Story:** In Lexington, two unity and reform movements, represented by Barton W Stone and Raccoon John Smith, come together to work out unity with each other. Yet after days of wrestling, they disagree on many foundational issues. The only basis for unity they can come up with is the Bible. And that's what they shake on.

**The Idea**: Our movement is united around a commitment to the Bible—NOT agreement on _interpretations_ of the Bible! While uniting on interpretations is impossible, uniting on commitment to wrestle with Scripture actually worked at least once.

   - Georgetown, autumn 1831: "agreed to meet and worship together" (CM 1832).
4. **The meeting: Hill Street, Lexington, Dec. 31, 1831.** "Not a meeting of Elders or Preachers only, but a popular assembly."
   - Smith's speech, built in: "one faith… ten thousand opinions"; "one in faith, and not in opinion"; "the Bible, and the Bible alone".
   - Stone: "never can be one in their speculations"; he offers his hand.
   - Rogers: "to abandon all speculations, especially on the Trinity".
   - Stone and Johnson: "we do not know, nor are we concerned to know"; the creed is "the New Testament".
   - *Option 1 lands here:* unity on the Bible, not on interpretations. Wright's "story authority" fits as the reading that makes this possible.
5. **The handshake, the song, the loaf.**
   - "A song arose…"; "On Lord's day, they broke the loaf together" (Williams).
   - "Though they still differed on… free or restricted communion, each felt that it was his privilege to commune with the other."
   - Contemporary witness: John Smith, Feb. 1832, "broke the loaf, and united in all the acts of social worship"; "what law of Christ is violated when we break the loaf together?"; Stone's editors' note, "Nor debar them."
6. **Campbell's welcome**: "renounced their former speculations"; "bid these brethren God speed"; and the circle: "all lines drawn from the circumference… will meet in the same point".
7. **The test: Lexington, February 1832.** *Option 6 lands here.*
   - Feb. 12: the two congregations pledge to "unite upon the New Testament"; hands and a song.
   - Feb. 19: they break the loaf together, with the preacher absent.
   - Feb. 25: the pledge is dissolved.
   - Why: "no person but a *preacher* has a right to administer the ordinances—such as *the breaking of the loaf*"; "alarmed at the idea of us common folks receiving the name of kings and priests to the Lord."
   - Coon's verdict: "It is the *Clergy*… that keep us apart." Babylon and Jerusalem.
8. **Campbell's verdict ("The Union", May 1832).** "Who ever read of a monthly breaking of the loaf, or of quarterly communion?"; "presented by the hands of one ordained by men"; "When he is absent the church can do nothing."
   - Side path: *Who keeps the table* (resources.py), back to Brush Run's ordination (Chapter 6).
9. **Union by patience.**
   - July 1835: the Lexington churches unite, the Christians waiving "clerical privilege".
   - Stone in Jacksonville (1834): "I refused to unite with either until they united together."
   - "The noblest act of my life."
10. **What the table teaches.**
    - Agreement on "the New Testament alone" did not settle what it allows. The unity held because they kept eating together, and it was finished when "us common folks" could serve.
    - Honest limits: Stone's ordained-hands view was about order; weekly communion could itself become a test.
11. **Ideas, discussion, site.**
