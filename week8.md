# Chapter 8 — The Stone-Campbell Merger (1832)

[← All chapters](.)

**Slides:** [Chapter 8 deck](decks/chapter8.html) (draft, Nov 20; opens with the quiz "No creed but…"; side paths: "Joined, refused, dissolved", "Who keeps the table", "No creed but…"; the map of the union spreading, 1831–1835)

**The Story:** In Lexington, two unity and reform movements, represented by Barton W Stone and Raccoon John Smith, come together to work out unity with each other. Yet after days of wrestling, they disagree on many foundational issues. The only basis for unity they can come up with is the Bible. And that's what they shake on.

**The Idea**: Our movement is united around a commitment to the Bible—NOT agreement on _interpretations_ of the Bible! While uniting on interpretations is impossible, uniting on commitment to wrestle with Scripture actually worked at least once.

---

## "Raccoon" John Smith, for flavor (2026-11-20; checked against Williams, *Life of Elder John Smith*, 1870, and the *Encyclopedia*, "Smith, 'Raccoon' John")

- **The frontier.** Born October 15, 1784, in Sullivan County, in what is now East Tennessee, to a Regular Baptist family; his father was the son of German immigrants (Smith, or Schmidt). Williams: "We ought, perhaps, to say, that he was a native of the State of Franklin, an unacknowledged, though duly organized commonwealth of the olden time" (p. 13). Franklin was the breakaway state, named for Benjamin Franklin, that the frontier counties formed in 1784–85 when North Carolina ceded them; it was never admitted, and it collapsed by 1788. Smith was born in the months it was taking shape. Raised in Stockton's Valley, under Poplar Mountain in what is now Clinton County, Kentucky, then "an almost unbroken solitude". He "never had more than six months of schooling" but was "a voracious reader and a diligent student of oratory" (*Encyclopedia*). At twelve he was sent alone through the wilderness with two pack horses to the nearest mill.
- **The fire, January 1815.** He had moved his family to the Hickory Flats near Huntsville, Alabama, dreaming of cotton land. While he was away preaching, a messenger came: "Your house took fire last night, and every thing is lost. Two of the children were burned to death" (Williams, p. 102). Riding home, the Calvinist preacher found he could not comfort his wife: "If I tell her that our babies are glorified, the thought that possibly they were of the non-elect will only aggravate her woe." His faith in that doctrine "yielded up its strength forever" (p. 103). Anna died soon after (1815). He remarried, to Nancy Hurt, and moved to Montgomery County, east of Lexington.
- **The name.** "Raccoon" meant backwoodsman: a man from the raccoon country of the Cumberland hills. It is not a comment on cunning or on looks.
  - **Where it began.** In 1815 he came back to Kentucky ragged and grieving after the fire, and preached to a Baptist association at Crab Orchard. He opened by saying where he was from: "I am John Smith, from Stockton's Valley. In more recent years, I have lived in Wayne, among the rocks and hills of the Cumberland. Down there, saltpeter caves abound, and raccoons make their homes. On that wild frontier we never had good schools, nor many books; consequently, I stand before you to-day a man without an education. But, my brethren, even in that ill-favored region, the Lord, in good time, found me" (Williams, p. 115; read on the page scan).
  - **How it spread.** The people wanted to hear again "the man that had been raised among the raccoons", and smiled at "his origin among the raccoons and caves of the rocky frontier" (p. 117).
  - **Where it stuck.** Years later, at Frankfort, Philip Fall, who had probably heard the Crab Orchard sermon, posted notices that "Raccoon John Smith would preach". From then on "some knew me as 'Raccoon John Smith, of Montgomery'" (pp. 277–278). He "disliked but tolerated" it, and the *Encyclopedia* notes "much disagreement over where and when" he got it.
- **The preacher.**
  - A deep, chant-like voice.
  - In 1828, more than thirty baptisms a week for months. To his wife: "I have baptized seven hundred sinners and capsized fifteen hundred Baptists" (p. 258).
  - He farmed his whole life, because the Baptists did not pay their preachers.
- **"A civil Campbellite"** (Williams, pp. 478–479, read on the page scan). On a journey south, a woman serving him breakfast asked what the "strange sort of people up there in Kentucky called Campbellites" looked like. She had heard they "even chased after people, and ran them down," and once ran a poor fellow "five miles before they caught him." Smith: "That was a very long-winded fellow, ma'am, to run five miles before the Campbellites caught him." She imagined "a sort of wild, fierce, fanatical look." Smith put down his knife and fork: "look right at me, and you will see one! But don't be afraid, I am a *civil* Campbellite, and will not chase *you* into the water."
- **His wit.** To a Reformer boasting that the last of his Calvinism was gone: "you may have drained out the last drop from the cask, but the very hoops and staves smell of it still!" (p. 431)
- **At Lexington.** Chosen by the Disciples to speak first. Afterwards he called the union "the best act of his life". To a brother who feared it would drive the Baptists further off: "certain I am that the union of Christians, upon a scriptural basis, is right, and that it can never be bad policy to do what is right" (p. 456).

So on slide 10, say "the Spirit" and "Christ", and keep "within the Trinity" for Christ only.
- **The date.** The meeting was Saturday, Dec. 31, 1831, at the start of a four-day meeting over New Year's 1832, with the loaf on Sunday, Jan. 1. "Lexington, 1832" is fine as shorthand.
- **Clintonville, Bourbon County, October 1832,** a general meeting that ratified the union, comes from Bower's 1962 history of Central Christian Church, via therestorationmovement.com. It is secondary: verify it before it goes on a slide.

**Walter Scott: the basis of "No creed but Christ"** (all checked against the extracted texts; OCR-only differences).
