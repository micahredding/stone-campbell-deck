# Chapter 6 — Wherever Two or Three Are Gathered: Brush Run Ordination

**The Story:** The fledgling unity movement sought to join with local Presbyterians, and was rebuffed. Now a church, Brush Run must ordain its own ministers and deacons, prompting a recognition that in the New Testament, authority inheres in congregations and in every believer. The priesthood of all believers is the root of Alexander Campbell's ministry.

**The Idea**: The Priesthood of All Believers points to real authority, inhering in every Christian, and conferred in baptism. Our movement embraces this deeply.

**Connections:**
- Chapter 5: the Synod of Pittsburg refused them; still seeking a larger union, they became a church. Brush Run was formed on May 4, 1811, and kept its first Supper the next day.
- Chapter 4: the Springfield Presbytery recognized the authority of the congregation to ordain its own ministers, as recognition of the work of the Spirit. Brush Run recognizes the same authority inhering in the congregation.

## The argument, as taught

**The ordination.** On January 1, 1812, the Brush Run church ordained Alexander Campbell "according to the principles of this Church." Thomas Campbell, whom the congregation had appointed elder the year before, signed the certificate as senior minister, with the four deacons the congregation had chosen. No bishop, no presbytery, no synod.

**Who gave them that authority?** Ask the room, and listen. It may not strike us as a big deal. In their world it was a very big deal, because whose ordinations are valid is finally a question of where rightful authority in the church comes from.

**Three answers.** Before the conflict, the positions: descent from a bishop, descent from a presbytery, or something else?

**Every church answered, and refused the next.** Constantinople rejects Rome, receiving Latins "as unordained and unbaptized" (1755). Rome rejects the Reformers (Trent, 1563: ministers "called and instituted by the people" are "thieves and robbers"), the Anglicans' orders ("absolutely null and utterly void," 1896), and the Protestants' (1964; 2007: not "Churches in the proper sense"). The Orthodox reject the Protestants: without a bishop "neither Church nor Christian" can be. Protestants reject each other: the Lutherans will not take the Zwinglians' Supper; the Anglicans reject the Presbyterians' orders, and the Methodists'; the Presbyterians call ordination by the people "null and void"; the Reformed drown an Anabaptist for gathering a church that is only the "cloak" of one; the Anabaptists answer that the Reformed are not sent; Massachusetts declares a Baptist church "no orderly church assembly"; and the Baptists reject everyone. Then: who possesses the authority? Every church has its line: bishops back to Peter, elders back to the apostles, Baptists back to John the Baptist (a joke, nearly true). Or something else?

**The question.** Is there any position that does not force us to say of large parts of Christianity that they have no valid orders, no valid ministers, no valid sacraments, and so are not churches at all? There is one: **the authority flows from the people.** If the people constitute themselves with elders, presbyters, bishops, independent congregations, or even a pope, we can debate the wisdom of the choice, but we need not call it illegitimate: not a false church, a false ministry, or false sacraments. All can be legitimate.

**This is Campbell's position.** If the people may constitute themselves under bishops, presbyters, elders or a pope, every church's ministers can be recognized. Read Campbell latest first, back to the winter of his ordination: "The community, the church, the multitude of the faithful, are the fountain of official power. This power descends from the body itself" (later); a community's officers and ordinances are "as divine and authoritative as any other… in any christian community on earth" (1843); "the clergy are the opposite to the laity," and both words mean God's people; "Every thing essential to appointment, call, or ordination was vested in the minds of the brethren" (1826); ordination "consists in the choice of the people, which must be hearty"; "Why do we contend for uninterrupted succession in ordination, seeing it is not the persons called bishops who have the power, but the people?" (1811).

**Don't get me wrong.** This is not the argument Campbell makes most of the time. He had seen Scotland, the independents (the Haldanes, Greville Ewing) against the established churches, and he usually argues that the clergy are corrupt and have monopolized power that belongs to the people. But there is an asymmetry. The established church is duty-bound to reject the independent's ministry: its doctrine and its institution require it. The independent is not bound to reject the established church's ministry; he can affirm his own and theirs. It is a one-way door. If established clergy want to recognize the independents' ministry as true (and in Campbell's view they ought), they can do it only by coming to the same conclusion: the authority inheres in the people, not in the episcopacy or the presbytery as such.

**Not new.** Elections have consequences; so do ordinations (the line now comes after the answer, "Christ"). Not new, and not ours alone. Stone's presbytery gave its power back to the congregations (1804); Luther (1520–23); the early church (Leo, Cyprian, Irenaeus, the *Didache*, *1 Clement*); and Jesus: the keys to Peter (Matthew 16), then, two chapters later, the same power to all the disciples gathered, with Christ in the midst of two or three (Matthew 18).

**In the Church of Christ.** A list, one click at a time: no titles (Thomas Campbell had already renounced "Reverend"; the Last Will: "our name of distinction, with its reverend title, be forgotten"); the preacher is "Brother"; members serve at the table; any Christian may baptize; the congregation picks its elders; each congregation governs itself; preaching schools, not seminaries; everyone sings. Then Brownlow (1945): "Each Christian is a priest." Much of it was men only: the ideal voiced, not yet extended. Beside unity, the movement's second great theme: the authority of Christ is vested in his people, wherever two or three are gathered.

**The landing.** So there is one logical position that lets us affirm the orders, ministries and sacraments of all Christendom without being bound by any of them, and it is the one our movement lands on through Campbell: the power to ordain and to minister begins with the people and flows from the people. "The community, the church, the multitude of the faithful, are the fountain of official power." It is an extraordinarily strong reading of the priesthood of all believers, and Campbell spent decades fighting for it: the church is constituted not by anyone else's authority but by Christ working through his people, gathered.

## Ideas

1. **The priesthood of all believers holds real authority.** Not a metaphor: the two or three gathered can make a minister. Brush Run ordained Alexander Campbell "according to the principles of this Church"; ordination "consists in the choice of the people"; "the multitude of the faithful, are the fountain of official power." Matthew 18: whatever you bind on earth.
2. **The priesthood of all believers lets us affirm the ministry of the whole church.** Every line of succession has to reject someone. If the authority is the people's, they may constitute themselves under bishops, presbyters or elders, and every one of those ministries can stand: we can debate the wisdom of each, and need not call any of them false.
3. **Ordinations have consequences.** How a church ordains says where it believes the authority lives: in a line of bishops, in a presbytery, or in the people. Make it a line, and you must reject whoever is outside it. Put it in the people, and every church's ministry can stand.

*Earlier notes:*

1. The priesthood of all believers points to real authority, inhering in every Christian, and conferred in baptism
2. The New Testament describes various forms of ordination. The power to ordain inheres in every ordained minister, in congregations of disciples, even in every believer called by the Spirit.
3. **"A taste of the Millennial Church."** Alexander Campbell says it of communion in his *first sermon* — the very sermon this ordination made possible. Do not let it pass as a pretty phrase; it is the engine of the entire movement, stated at the outset by the man who would spend fifty years working out its consequences. The table is not a memorial but a foretaste: God's future, arriving early. That is why the fences around it had to come down, why his father stood trial for opening it, and why a church would eventually hand the Supper to a farmer rather than reserve it to a priest. Everything radical in this tradition is downstream of what Campbell thought was happening at that table.
4. **The two conferrals are the same conferral.** Note what has just happened at Brush Run: a congregation with no external authorization ordains its own. And note where authority is said to come from — conferred in baptism, at the water, in front of everyone. The authority to ordain and the authority to preside at the table are not two powers; they are the one priesthood, handed to ordinary people. This is the hinge between the first half of the class and the second.
