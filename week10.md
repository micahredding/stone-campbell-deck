## Entering the Practices (Chapters 10–13)

Everything so far has been the story of how the freedom was won. What remains is the harder question: what holds a church together once it has refused to be held together from above?

If we take the priesthood of all believers seriously — if we allow no mediators — then the priesthood has to be *practiced*, and practiced constantly. An office persists between meetings; a practice doesn't. So a church that dissolved its offices had to become a church that assembles often and does something every time.

And notice what got chosen. Someone can preach at you, decide for you, administer to you. But no one can be baptized for you, sing for you, read the text for you, or vote for you. The practices at the center of this movement are precisely the acts that admit no proxy. That is not an accident of history; it is the whole design.

What those acts produce is more than individual participation. When you sing, you hear that everyone around you has a voice. When you study, you know the person beside you can check the preacher's citation. Each practice performs your own priesthood and witnesses to everyone else's — and everyone knows that everyone knows. That mutual, public, repeated knowledge is the binding. It is what this movement has instead of a hierarchy, and the four chapters ahead are four different ways of doing it.

And the clearest case is the one practice that gets no week of its own, because it gets every week. In Chapter 1 we asked the table's first question — *who may come?* — and watched the test at the door get withdrawn. Here is its second question: *who may preside?* In the Catholic tradition that question is the definition of priesthood itself; the priest is the one who may stand at the altar, and the layman is the one who may not. Which means a church that hands the Supper to a farmer, a teenager, a man who fixes cars all week — and does it every Sunday, as a matter of course, without anyone thinking it remarkable — is not using "priesthood of all believers" as a slogan. It is the most literal enactment of that doctrine anywhere in Protestantism. Nearly everyone else said it and then kept an ordained man at the table for the sake of good order. We said it and meant it, fifty-two times a year, in front of everybody.

So the hospitality of the early chapters and the priesthood of these later ones are not two themes. They are one table, asked two questions, answered the same way both times: no one stands between you and it.

> **What a priest is — said carefully, by people who still believe it.** This is worth hearing in their own words, because it is not a strawman and it is not a small claim. The Catechism of the Catholic Church puts it in one sentence: *"Only validly ordained priests can preside at the Eucharist and consecrate the bread and the wine so that they become the Body and Blood of the Lord"* (§1411). And the Second Vatican Council is the fairest statement of the difference, because it grants almost everything we would want to say — there really is a "common priesthood of the faithful," it says, and then: the common priesthood and the ministerial priesthood *"differ from one another in essence and not only in degree,"* because "the ministerial priest, by the sacred power he enjoys, teaches and rules the priestly people; acting in the person of Christ, he makes present the Eucharistic sacrifice" (*Lumen Gentium* 10). Four centuries earlier the Council of Trent had drawn the line harder, defining priesthood by the altar rather than the pulpit: against any claim that the New Testament has no priesthood but "only an office and bare ministry of preaching the Gospel" (Session 23, Canon 1). So: Rome affirms the priesthood of all believers *and* reserves the altar. We affirm it and open the altar. That difference is the entire second half of this class.

And one more thing follows, and it is the honest ending rather than a triumphant one. If the priesthood belongs to every believer, and if it is conferred in baptism — which is where this very chapter begins — then *any* fence at the head of the table is precisely the sort of human addition this movement existed to remove. For most of our history "any member may preside" quietly meant *any man*. That this congregation now sets no such fence is not a modern borrowing grafted onto the tradition; it is the tradition's own founding logic finally cashed out — one more fence found and removed, by the same reasoning that removed all the others. Which should keep us from congratulating ourselves. The doctrine has always been more radical than the people holding it, and it is very likely still out ahead of us.

**The Story:** On the frontier, thousands sat in agony at the mourner's bench, waiting for a feeling that would prove they were saved. Walter Scott discovered an answer: a gospel you could respond to *today*. This was a liberation. God had provided a path of assurance — you can act, and in acting, know that God has forgiven you.

**The Idea**: Believer's Baptism embodies the Priesthood of All Believers right at the start. God empowers our own agency as the medium to express the assurance of salvation.

**Connections:**
- Back to Cane Ridge: the ecstatic experience of Chapter 1 has hardened into a boundary — the required experience as a new fence. Stone overcame the Presbyterian boundary by calling sinners simply to "come"; now that same overcoming is needed again, at the water.
- Believer's baptism elevates agency and consent — the priesthood of all believers at the moment of entry. God won't force you, and he also will not deny you.

**The Thread — the practice that *confers*:** Baptism is the one practice that happens only once, because it is the conferral itself: the moment the church's whole authority is placed in an ordinary person. No one can be baptized on your behalf; the agency has to be yours or the act is empty. And the congregation watches it happen — everyone present sees a new priest made, and knows that everyone else saw it too. Every practice that follows maintains the standing this one confers.

## Narrative

*Draft, worked in from the June 2026 Baptism Storyline. To rework.*

Tell it as one man, two moments, twenty-five years apart: **a creek in 1812** and **a letter in 1837**. The first gives us our conviction about baptism. The second gives us our character.

### The creek (June 12, 1812)

Alexander Campbell is twenty-three. His first child, Jane, has just been born, and a question that had been comfortably theoretical — is infant baptism scriptural? — is now sitting in a cradle in his house. He does what he will do his whole life: he goes to the text as if he had never read it before, and follows it wherever it leads.

Where it leads is uncomfortable. The son of a Presbyterian minister, a minister himself, concludes that he has never been baptized.

He goes to a Baptist preacher, Matthias Luce, and asks to be immersed. But Baptist practice required a candidate first to narrate a *conversion experience* before the congregation — proof that grace had already happened to you, so that baptism could testify to it. Campbell refuses. That requirement, he tells Luce, is a human rule with no scriptural warrant. He will be baptized the way the first Christians were: on the simple confession that Jesus is the Messiah, the Son of God. Luce hesitates — this could cost him with his association — and then agrees.

Seven went into Buffalo Creek that day, Thomas Campbell among them: the father following the son.

Notice what actually happened. The movement's very first act of baptismal conviction was the *removal* of a man-made barrier between a believer and the water. Before we ever argued about baptism, we simplified access to it. The first thing this practice did among us was open a gate.

### The discovery (1820–1827)

Campbell's understanding deepened in public. In his 1820 debate with John Walker he still held the usual Baptist view: baptism is a sign of a forgiveness that has already happened inside you. By the McCalla debate in 1823 he had seen something in Acts he could not unsee — "Repent, and be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ **for the remission of sins**" (Acts 2:38). Baptism is not the receipt for a transaction made somewhere else. It is where God's forgiveness is pledged to you.

Then Walter Scott carried it onto the Western Reserve in 1827 and found out what it did *pastorally*. Revivalism had left people in agony at the mourner's bench, waiting for a feeling — an inner lightning strike that would prove they were saved. Scott offered what no revivalist could: a gospel you could respond to *today*. Faith, repentance, baptism, remission of sins, the gift of the Holy Spirit. You did not have to wait for an experience to be manufactured in you. You could come to the water tonight and stand on God's promise instead of your own emotions.

That is what it was at its birth: a doctrine of **assurance**. God's objective pledge, replacing the tyranny of feeling.

### The letter (1837)

Then the conviction was tested — from inside.

John Thomas, a physician and editor in the movement, pushed the doctrine one step further than Campbell ever had: if baptism is for the remission of sins, a baptism not consciously *understood* that way must be invalid. He began re-immersing Baptists, and his partisans went further still, treating the unimmersed as no Christians at all.

In that air Campbell received a letter from "a conscientious sister" in Lunenburg, Virginia. She had read him saying that he found in all Protestant parties Christians as exemplary as ourselves, and she meant to pin him down: how does anyone become a Christian? Does the name belong to any but those who are buried by baptism into the death of Christ?

The trap was built out of his own teaching. The best-known advocate of believers' immersion in America was being asked to declare most of the Christian world outside of Christ — and his own logic, pushed one step, seemed to demand it.

He refused. Read it aloud:

> "But who is a Christian? I answer, Every one that believes in his heart that Jesus of Nazareth is the Messiah, the Son of God; repents of his sins, and obeys him in all things according to his measure of knowledge of his will."

> "I cannot, therefore, make any one duty the standard of Christian state or character, **not even immersion**."

> "Should I find a Pedobaptist more intelligent in the Christian Scriptures, more spiritually-minded and more devoted to the Lord than a Baptist, or one immersed on a profession of the ancient faith, I could not hesitate a moment in giving the preference of my heart to him that loveth most. Did I act otherwise, I would be a pure sectarian, **a Pharisee among Christians**."

> "It is the image of Christ the Christian looks for and loves; and this does not consist in being exact in a few items, but in general devotion to the whole truth as far as known."

And he did not lower his view of baptism by an inch to say it. In the same articles: immersion is not "absolutely essential to a Christian — though it may be greatly essential to his sanctification and comfort."

So the legalistic version — *your baptism didn't count* — is not the original teaching that later generations softened. It is a deviation the founder himself named and rejected, in print, at the height of his influence. When we hold a high view of baptism without unchristianizing our neighbors, we are not compromising the tradition. We are keeping it. John Thomas went on to found a separate body, the Christadelphians. Campbell stayed.

### The homecoming

One more turn. What Campbell found in the water was not a new doctrine. It was the oldest one.

The Nicene Creed, confessed for sixteen centuries by Orthodox, Catholic, Anglican and Lutheran Christians, says: **"We acknowledge one baptism for the remission of sins."** That is Acts 2:38's own phrase. Campbell, reading Acts on the frontier with no creed in his hand, arrived where the whole church had always confessed. The view he left behind — baptism as a bare symbol of a salvation settled elsewhere — was the recent arrival.

And the creeds themselves grew out of baptism. Among the earliest we have is the question asked at the water, recorded in the *Apostolic Tradition* (early third century): *Do you believe in Christ Jesus, the Son of God…?* Campbell's single question at the creek is that same confession. We never stopped confessing the ancient faith. We confess it the original way: one believer at a time, standing in the water, with the church watching.

Tell it as a homecoming, not a victory. A frontier movement that gave up names, creeds and institutions found that its plain reading of Scripture had carried it into the company of the whole church.

### The idea: bad, good, redeemed

- **The legalistic version (name it honestly):** baptism as transaction and test — validity hinging on the right mode, the right formula, the right understanding at the right moment. Identity policed at the waterline. It is real, it did pastoral damage, and people in the room have been hurt by it. Say so.
- **The deeper version:** baptism is real because *God* acts in it. "Baptism is not a human work; it is the place where God does the work that only God can do" (*Encyclopedia of the Stone-Campbell Movement*). The gospel enacted on one body on one day; God's pledge, not your performance.
- **The redeemed version:** hold both Campbells together — the 1812 Campbell who would let no human rule stand between a believer and the water, and the 1837 Campbell who would not let baptism itself become such a rule. Conviction without coercion.

## Ideas

1. **Assurance, not performance.** Baptism is real because God acts in it — a pledge you can stand on, against every mourner's bench, then and now.
2. **Assurance is agency.** The first experience of conversion is your own choice — your agency validated in the deepest sense. God won't force you, and he will not deny you.
3. **The gate was opened before the gospel was preached through it.** The movement's first baptismal act removed a man-made barrier between a believer and the water.
4. **Hold both Campbells together.** No human rule between a believer and the water (1812) — and baptism itself must never become such a rule (1837): "not even immersion… a Pharisee among Christians."
5. **Most distinctive, most catholic.** "One baptism for the remission of sins" is the Nicene Creed's own confession — reaching for the original, we found ourselves holding the inheritance.

## Future

*What is beautiful here? What do we carry forward?*

- **An embodied gospel for a disembodied age.** Everything around us is mediated, virtual and revisable. Baptism is unrepeatable, physical and public: a body goes under, a date goes on a calendar, a community witnesses.
- **Assurance without anxiety.** The mourner's bench just moved online. Scott's discovery still preaches: God has given you something to stand on other than the quality of your feelings.
- **A doorway into the whole inheritance.** The practice that seemed to set us apart joins us to the creeds and the fathers. What else in our inheritance is richer than we were told?
- **Conviction without coercion is our actual heritage.** A mixed-tradition congregation doesn't need us to go soft on baptism, or to re-litigate anyone's past. It needs what Campbell modeled under pressure: teach the full conviction, honor the work of God in every life, and make no one duty the standard of Christian character.

## Discussion Questions

1. Campbell's first baptismal conviction removed a barrier: the required conversion narrative. Where do we still ask for the equivalent — proof of an inner experience — before treating someone as fully belonging?
2. Scott offered the frontier *assurance*: a promise you could act on, instead of a feeling you had to wait for. What is the modern mourner's bench, and what would baptismal assurance sound like preached to it?
3. The Lunenburg trap was built from Campbell's own teaching pushed one step too far. Which of our teachings are one step from becoming weapons, and how do we take that step back?
4. If "one baptism for the remission of sins" puts us with the Nicene Creed — and weekly communion with Justin Martyr, and our singing with the ancient church — what else might we share with the deep Christian tradition that we assumed made us different?
